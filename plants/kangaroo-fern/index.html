About the Kangaroo Fern

Unusual long fronds with prominent veining. Easier than most ferns.

Origin: Australia

Care Tips

Low to medium light, keep moist, tolerates lower humidity.

Kangaroo Fern Care in Depth

Light

Kangaroo fern wants medium indirect light and tolerates lower light well. It grows as an epiphyte and ground-creeper in Australian and New Zealand forests, adapted to dappled shade beneath the canopy.

A north or east window suits it well. Direct sun scorches the glossy fronds and bleaches their deep green. In deep shade it survives but produces fewer new fronds. Like blue star fern, this is one of the more forgiving ferns about light, and light problems are far less common with it than with maidenhair.

Watering through the year

Water when the top inch of mix has dried, then water thoroughly and drain fully. In spring and summer that is usually every five to seven days; in winter, every ten days or so.

Kangaroo fern wants consistent moisture but is more tolerant of brief dryness than most true ferns, thanks to its fleshy creeping rhizome which stores some water. It is also more prone to root rot than to drying out, so avoid keeping it soggy.

Humidity above 50% is preferred, and dry air shows as browning frond edges. It is sensitive to salts and chlorine in tap water, so filtered or rainwater is worth using. A bathroom or kitchen position often suits it well.

Soil and potting mix

As an epiphyte, kangaroo fern wants a loose, airy medium rather than dense compost — a mix of orchid bark, perlite and coir or peat works well. Ordinary potting soil suffocates the creeping rhizome and causes rot.

A shallow, wide pot suits the surface-creeping rhizome far better than a deep one. Feed at quarter to half strength monthly through spring and summer only — ferns are sensitive to fertiliser salts, and excess shows as browned frond edges.

How to Propagate Kangaroo Fern

Kangaroo fern is propagated by dividing the rhizome, which is straightforward and far easier than spore propagation.

The rhizome is the furry creeping stem that runs across the surface of the medium with fronds emerging at intervals. It should sit on top of the mix rather than being buried — this is the key point with all rhizomatous ferns.

In spring, slide the plant out and cut the rhizome into sections with a clean knife, making sure each piece has at least two or three fronds and some roots beneath it. Lay each section on top of fresh bark-based mix, pressing it lightly into contact without burying it, and pin it down with a bent wire if it will not stay put. Water lightly, keep humidity up for a few weeks, and new fronds appear within a month or two. Spore propagation is possible but slow and unnecessary given how reliably division works.

Common Kangaroo Fern Problems & Fixes

Brown frond edges and tips

Usually means: Salts or chlorine in tap water, low humidity, or over-fertilising.

What to do: Switch to filtered or rainwater, flush the pot with clean water every couple of months, and reduce feeding to quarter strength. Trim damaged edges following the frond shape.

Fronds yellowing with the medium staying wet

Usually means: Overwatering and rhizome rot — the main way this fern is killed, since people water it like a Boston fern.

What to do: Let the top inch dry properly between waterings. Check the rhizome: firm and furry is healthy, soft and brown is rot. Repot into a chunkier bark mix.

Rhizome buried and rotting

Usually means: Planted too deep. The creeping rhizome must sit on the surface.

What to do: Repot with the rhizome exposed on top of the mix. This is the most common potting error with rhizomatous ferns.

Fronds pale and bleached

Usually means: Too much light, usually direct sun.

What to do: Move to a shadier position. Colour returns on new growth.

Brown dots in rows on frond undersides

Usually means: Spores, which are normal on a mature fern and a sign of health.

What to do: No action needed. Irregularly scattered bumps that scrape off are scale insects instead.

New fronds small or deformed

Usually means: Low humidity, over-fertilising, or physical damage to the delicate unfurling fronds.

What to do: Raise humidity, reduce feeding, and avoid brushing past new growth as it unfurls.

Whole fronds browning and dying back

Usually means: Severe drought, or rhizome rot at the other extreme.

What to do: Check the rhizome and medium. Dry and shrunken means soak the pot thoroughly; wet and soft means cut away rot and repot into an airier mix.

Repotting Kangaroo Fern

Repot every two to three years in spring, or when the rhizome has crept across the whole surface and over the pot edge.

Choose a shallow, wide pot — depth is wasted on a surface-creeping rhizome and simply holds wet medium. Use fresh bark-based epiphytic mix.

The critical point: lay the rhizome on top of the mix rather than burying it, with only the roots below the surface. Water lightly, keep humidity up for a few weeks, and wait six weeks before feeding. This is the natural moment to divide a plant that has outgrown its pot.

Kangaroo Fern Varieties to Look For

Microsorum diversifolium is the kangaroo fern or kangaroo paw fern, with glossy dark green fronds that vary in shape on the same plant — young fronds are simple and undivided, mature ones become lobed like a paw, which is where the name comes from.

Microsorum musifolium 'Crocodyllus', the crocodile fern, is a related species with broad fronds and a distinctive reticulated vein pattern. Blue star fern, Phlebodium aureum, has a similar creeping rhizome and similar care needs. Compared with Boston or maidenhair ferns, kangaroo fern is considerably more tolerant of dry air and irregular watering. All Microsorum are non-toxic to cats and dogs.
