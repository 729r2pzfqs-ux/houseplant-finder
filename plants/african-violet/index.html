About the African Violet

Beloved flowering houseplant in purple, pink, white. Blooms repeatedly.

Origin: East Africa

Care Tips

Bright indirect light, water from below, avoid wet leaves.

African Violet Care in Depth

Light

African violet needs bright indirect light to flower, and light is almost always the reason one stops blooming. Twelve to fourteen hours of bright indirect light daily is what produces continuous flowering — which is why they do so well under grow lights.

An east or north window is ideal. A south or west window needs a sheer curtain, since direct sun scorches the fuzzy leaves and leaves pale bleached patches. The leaves indicate the light level: healthy plants have flat leaves held horizontally. Leaves reaching upward mean too little light; leaves curling down over the pot rim mean too much.

Watering through the year

The single most important rule is to keep water off the leaves. The fine hairs trap water, and droplets sitting on the foliage in cool conditions cause the pale ring-shaped spots that disfigure so many African violets. Water from below by standing the pot in a saucer of tepid water for twenty to thirty minutes, then pouring away what is left.

Water when the surface feels barely dry, usually every five to seven days. Never let the plant wilt, and never leave it standing in water. Use tepid rather than cold water — cold water on the roots also causes leaf spotting.

Humidity of 50% or more suits them. A pebble tray works well; misting does not, for the obvious reason that it puts water on the leaves. Self-watering African violet pots, which wick moisture up from a reservoir, solve most of these problems at once and are worth using.

Soil and potting mix

Use a light, airy African violet mix, or make your own from equal parts peat or coir, perlite and vermiculite. Standard potting compost is too dense and holds too much water around the fine roots.

Use a small, shallow pot. African violets have shallow root systems and flower best when slightly pot-bound — the general rule is a pot about one third the diameter of the leaf spread. Feed with a balanced or high-phosphorus African violet fertiliser at quarter strength with most waterings, which suits continuous flowering better than occasional heavy feeding.

How to Propagate African Violet

African violets propagate reliably from leaf cuttings, and this is one of the most rewarding propagations available — a single leaf produces a cluster of plantlets.

Choose a healthy leaf from the middle rows of the rosette rather than the oldest outer ones or the newest centre ones. Cut the petiole to about an inch long, at a 45-degree angle. Insert the petiole into damp, light propagation mix at a shallow angle so the leaf blade sits just above the surface, and cover with a clear bag or propagator to hold humidity.

Keep warm in bright indirect light. Roots form in three to four weeks, and a cluster of tiny plantlets emerges at the base of the petiole after two to three months. Once they have several leaves each, separate them carefully and pot individually. One leaf typically yields three to six plants. Note that variegated cultivars may not come true from leaf cuttings, and chimera varieties with striped flowers never do — those must be propagated from suckers instead.

Common African Violet Problems & Fixes

Pale rings or blotches on the leaves

Usually means: Water droplets sitting on the fuzzy leaves, particularly cold water, causing cell damage where the sun hits them.

What to do: Water from below only and use tepid water. The marks are permanent on affected leaves; remove the worst and new growth will be clean.

No flowers for months

Usually means: Insufficient light most commonly, then too large a pot, too much nitrogen, or a plant that needs repotting.

What to do: Give twelve to fourteen hours of bright indirect light daily, or use a grow light. Keep the pot small, and feed with a higher-phosphorus fertiliser.

Leaf stalks reaching upward, leaves stacked vertically

Usually means: Too little light. The plant is stretching toward the source.

What to do: Move to a brighter position or add a grow light. Rotate the pot weekly so the rosette stays symmetrical.

Leaves curling downward over the pot rim

Usually means: Too much light, or cold temperatures.

What to do: Filter the light with a sheer curtain and keep the plant above 18°C, away from cold windowsills.

Crown rotting, centre leaves mushy

Usually means: Water sitting in the crown, or the plant being buried too deep at repotting.

What to do: Usually fatal once the growing point goes. Take leaf cuttings immediately from healthy outer leaves. Always water from below, and set the crown at the surface when repotting.

A bare woody stem lifting the rosette above the pot

Usually means: Normal ageing — African violets develop a neck as lower leaves are removed over time.

What to do: Repot deeper: remove the lower leaves, scrape the neck lightly, and bury it so the rosette sits at soil level. The buried neck produces new roots.

Distorted, tightly bunched centre growth

Usually means: Cyclamen mites, which are microscopic and attack the growing point.

What to do: Difficult to treat and highly contagious. Isolate the plant immediately. Taking clean leaf cuttings from unaffected outer leaves and discarding the parent is usually the most practical course.

Repotting African Violet

Repot every six to twelve months, which is more often than most houseplants. African violets exhaust their mix quickly and the soil compacts around the fine roots, which reduces flowering.

Use the same size pot or only slightly larger — the rule of thumb is a pot about one third the diameter of the leaf spread. Refreshing the mix matters more than adding space.

Remove the lower row of leaves, scrape the exposed neck gently, and set the plant deeper so the crown sits at soil level. Water from below and keep humidity up for a week. Remove any suckers — side rosettes that form in the leaf axils — since they divert energy from flowering, though they can be potted separately.

African Violet Varieties to Look For

Saintpaulia, now reclassified as Streptocarpus sect. Saintpaulia, comes in thousands of registered cultivars. Standard varieties spread eight to sixteen inches, semi-miniatures six to eight, and miniatures under six — the small ones are particularly well suited to windowsills.

Flowers range from single to double, in purple, pink, white, blue and bicolours, with ruffled and edged forms. Chimera varieties have pinwheel stripes and can only be propagated from suckers, never leaves. Trailing varieties have multiple crowns rather than a single rosette. All African violets are non-toxic to cats and dogs, making them one of the safest flowering houseplants.
