About the Bird of Paradise

Dramatic tropical plant with large paddle-shaped leaves. Can produce stunning flowers.

Origin: South Africa

Care Tips

Bright direct light, regular watering, feed monthly.

Bird of Paradise Care in Depth

Light

Bird of paradise is a full-sun plant in its native South Africa and needs the brightest position you can offer indoors. Several hours of direct sun daily, ideally from a south or west-facing window, is what produces strong upright growth and the large paddle leaves.

This is not a low-light plant, despite sometimes being sold as one. In insufficient light it produces small leaves on long weak stems and will never flower. Indoor flowering requires a mature plant — typically four to five years old — in excellent light, and many indoor specimens never bloom at all, which is worth knowing before you buy one for the flowers.

Watering through the year

Water thoroughly when the top two inches of mix have dried, letting water run from the drainage holes. In spring and summer, in a bright spot, that is usually every seven to ten days. These are large plants that transpire a lot of water in good light.

Reduce substantially in autumn and winter, to every two to three weeks. The combination of lower light and cooler temperatures means the mix stays wet far longer, and winter overwatering is the most common cause of root problems.

Bird of paradise appreciates humidity above 50% and shows dry air as browning leaf edges. It is also sensitive to salts and fluoride in tap water, which contribute to the same symptom, so filtered or rainwater is worth trying if edges keep browning despite good humidity.

Soil and potting mix

Use a rich, well-draining mix: roughly two parts quality potting compost to one part perlite and one part bark. The plant is a heavy feeder with vigorous thick roots and wants a medium with both substance and drainage.

Choose a sturdy, heavy pot — a mature bird of paradise is tall and top-heavy, and the root system is powerful enough to crack thin plastic. Feed every two to four weeks through spring and summer with a balanced fertiliser; this is one of the hungrier houseplants and responds visibly to regular feeding. Stop over winter.

How to Propagate Bird of Paradise

Bird of paradise is propagated by division, which is the only practical home method. Seed is possible but germination is slow and erratic, and a seed-grown plant takes four to five years to reach flowering size.

Divide in spring, ideally while repotting a mature clump. Slide the plant out and look for natural divisions where a fan of leaves has its own rhizome and root mass. The roots are thick, fleshy and densely packed, so a clean sharp knife or even a saw is usually needed rather than teasing them apart. Each division needs at least a fan or two of leaves and a substantial share of roots. Pot into fresh rich mix, water well, and keep in bright indirect light rather than full sun for the first month while the cut roots heal. Expect the division to sulk for several weeks and possibly lose a leaf. Note that dividing sets back flowering considerably, so if your plant blooms, think twice before splitting it.

Common Bird of Paradise Problems & Fixes

Leaves splitting along their length

Usually means: Entirely normal. The splits are an adaptation that lets wind pass through the large leaves in the wild, and indoor plants split as the leaves mature and are brushed against.

What to do: No action needed. Excessive splitting on young leaves can indicate low humidity or a plant in a high-traffic spot, so move it somewhere less brushed past if it bothers you.

Brown, crispy leaf edges

Usually means: Low humidity, salts and fluoride in tap water, or inconsistent watering.

What to do: Raise humidity, switch to filtered or rainwater, and flush the pot with plain water every few months. Water on a more regular schedule.

Yellowing lower leaves

Usually means: Overwatering if widespread and the mix is wet; normal ageing if it is the oldest leaf one at a time.

What to do: Let the top two inches dry between waterings, particularly in winter. Cut spent leaves off at the base.

No flowers on a mature plant

Usually means: Insufficient light is the usual reason, followed by insufficient feeding, too large a pot, or a plant that is simply not old enough.

What to do: Move it to the brightest available position and feed regularly through the growing season. Keep it slightly pot-bound, which encourages flowering. Be realistic: many indoor plants never flower.

New leaves emerging small and pale

Usually means: Too little light, a shortage of nutrients, or a root system that has outgrown its pot.

What to do: Increase light, feed through spring and summer, and repot if roots have filled the container.

Fine webbing under leaves, stippled surfaces

Usually means: Spider mites, which colonise the broad undersides where they go unnoticed for weeks.

What to do: Wipe both sides of every leaf, rinse the plant, and treat with insecticidal soap every five days for three cycles. Raising humidity discourages them.

Leaves curling and drooping with wet soil

Usually means: Root rot from overwatering, which prevents the roots taking up water even though the mix is saturated.

What to do: Unpot and inspect. Trim every soft brown root, repot into fresh free-draining mix, and water sparingly until new growth appears.

Repotting Bird of Paradise

Repot every two to three years while the plant is building size, then less often. Bird of paradise has thick, vigorous roots that will visibly deform or split a plastic pot when it is genuinely ready.

Repot in spring into a pot one size larger, choosing something heavy and stable — a large specimen is top-heavy and a light pot will tip. Ceramic or a weighted planter is worth the investment.

There is a trade-off worth understanding: this plant flowers best when slightly pot-bound, so if flowering is your goal, resist moving it up too eagerly and top-dress with fresh compost instead. Water well after repotting and wait six weeks before feeding. For very large plants, replacing the top few inches of mix each spring is more practical than repotting at all.

Bird of Paradise Varieties to Look For

Strelitzia reginae is the orange bird of paradise, reaching four to six feet indoors with the familiar orange and blue flowers. Strelitzia nicolai, the giant white bird of paradise, is much larger — easily reaching the ceiling — with white and blue flowers and broader leaves; it is the one most often sold as a large architectural houseplant and it very rarely flowers indoors.

Strelitzia juncea has narrow, almost reed-like leaves and a sculptural habit. All Strelitzia are toxic to cats and dogs, with the flowers and seeds being the most hazardous parts. Given the size these plants reach, the practical constraint indoors is usually ceiling height and light, not care difficulty.
