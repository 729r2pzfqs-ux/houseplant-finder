About the Hoya Kerrii

Famous for its perfect heart-shaped leaves. Often sold as single-leaf cuttings for Valentines Day, but can grow into a full vine.

Origin: Southeast Asia

Care Tips

Bright indirect light, let soil dry completely between waterings, very drought tolerant.

Hoya Kerrii Care in Depth

Light

Hoya kerrii wants bright indirect light with some gentle direct morning sun. Like other hoyas, light is the main determinant of whether it ever flowers, and it needs good light simply to grow at a reasonable pace.

An east window is ideal, or a south or west window with the plant set back or filtered. Harsh afternoon sun scorches the thick heart-shaped leaves. The variegated forms need noticeably more light than the plain green to hold their cream and yellow markings. In medium light the plant survives almost indefinitely but grows very slowly and will not bloom.

Watering through the year

The thick waxy leaves are substantial water stores, so treat this as close to a succulent. Let the top two inches of mix dry out before watering, then water thoroughly and drain fully. In spring and summer that is usually every two weeks; in winter, every three to four weeks.

Slightly wrinkled leaves mean it is ready for water. Overwatering is the main cause of failure and shows as yellowing leaves and a blackened stem base.

The single-leaf plants sold as novelties need particular care here: with no stem and minimal roots, they use almost no water, and watering one on a normal houseplant schedule will rot it within months. Water a single leaf sparingly — once a month is often enough. Humidity above 50% is appreciated but not critical.

Soil and potting mix

Hoya kerrii is an epiphyte and wants a chunky, fast-draining medium — one part potting compost, one part orchid bark and one part perlite. Some growers use straight orchid bark with good results.

Keep the pot small; hoyas flower better pot-bound and rot in an oversized pot of wet medium. Feed monthly at half strength through spring and summer, using a higher-phosphorus fertiliser if you want to encourage flowering. Stop in winter. Provide a trellis or support for a vining plant, since kerrii climbs given the chance.

How to Propagate Hoya Kerrii

This is the most important thing to know about Hoya kerrii, and it accounts for a great deal of disappointment: the single heart-shaped leaf sold in a pot, usually around Valentine's Day, will almost never grow into a plant.

That leaf was cut without a node. It will root, and it will sit there looking healthy and green for years — sometimes a decade — but with no node it has no growth point and can never produce a vine. It is a living ornament, not a young plant. If you want a leaf that will grow, look for one with a short piece of stem and a visible node attached, which some sellers do offer.

To propagate a vining plant properly, take a cutting with at least one node and one or two leaves, cutting just below the node. Root in water, damp sphagnum moss or perlite, keeping it warm and humid. Roots take four to eight weeks — hoyas are slow. Never cut off the woody flower spurs, since hoyas rebloom from the same peduncle year after year.

Common Hoya Kerrii Problems & Fixes

A single leaf that never grows

Usually means: It was cut without a node, so it has no growth point. This is by far the most common Hoya kerrii question.

What to do: There is no fix — the leaf cannot produce a vine. Keep it as an ornament, water it very sparingly, and buy a vining plant separately if you want one that grows.

Leaves yellowing with damp mix

Usually means: Overwatering and the beginnings of root rot, particularly likely in single-leaf plants.

What to do: Let the medium dry out thoroughly between waterings, and water single leaves no more than monthly. Repot into a chunkier, faster-draining mix.

Leaves wrinkled and soft

Usually means: Underwatering, or root damage preventing uptake.

What to do: Water thoroughly. If the leaves do not firm up within a week and the medium is damp, check the roots for rot.

Never flowers

Usually means: Insufficient light, a plant too young, too large a pot, or removed flower spurs.

What to do: Move to bright indirect light, keep it pot-bound, and feed with a higher-phosphorus fertiliser. Never cut the woody spurs where flowers appeared — they rebloom from the same point.

Long bare vine with no leaves

Usually means: Normal. Hoyas send out bare searching tendrils before producing leaves along them.

What to do: Leave them alone — cutting them removes future growth. Give the vine something to twine around.

Variegation fading to plain green

Usually means: Too little light for the variegated cultivar.

What to do: Move brighter. Note that heavily variegated leaves with little green grow very slowly, since they have less chlorophyll.

White cottony clusters in the leaf joints

Usually means: Mealybugs, the most common hoya pest.

What to do: Dab each with rubbing alcohol on a cotton bud and inspect weekly for a month.

Repotting Hoya Kerrii

Hoyas flower best pot-bound, so repot rarely — every three years at most, or when the medium has broken down and stopped draining.

Move up one small size only, or simply refresh the same pot with new mix. Spring is best. The roots are fine and sparse, so handle gently.

Single-leaf plants essentially never need repotting; their root systems stay minimal. For vining plants, be careful of the twining stems, which tangle and snap. Water lightly afterwards and wait a month before feeding. If your hoya is flowering, leave it alone.

Hoya Kerrii Varieties to Look For

Hoya kerrii is the sweetheart hoya or Valentine hoya, with thick heart-shaped leaves on a climbing vine. 'Variegata' has cream margins, and 'Albomarginata' and 'Reverse Variegata' offer different variegation patterns — all slower than the plain green form.

Within the genus, H. carnosa is the classic wax plant with clusters of scented pink flowers, 'Compacta' is the Hindu rope with tightly curled leaves, and H. pubicalyx and H. obovata are both easy and free-flowering. All hoyas are non-toxic to cats and dogs, which makes them one of the safest choices for a trailing or climbing plant in a pet household.
