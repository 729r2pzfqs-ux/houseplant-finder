About the Zebra Haworthia

Small rosette with white striped dark green leaves. Perfect desk plant.

Origin: South Africa

Care Tips

Bright indirect light, water sparingly, well-draining soil.

Zebra Haworthia Care in Depth

Light

Zebra haworthia wants bright indirect light, and this is where it differs usefully from most succulents. In habitat it grows in the shade of rocks and shrubs in South Africa rather than fully exposed, so it tolerates less light than an echeveria or aloe and actually scorches in intense direct sun.

An east-facing windowsill is close to ideal. A south or west window works with a sheer curtain. In too much sun the leaves turn red-brown or bleach white; in too little they stretch upward, the rosette opens out and the white banding dulls. The compact, tightly stacked rosette is the sign light levels are right.

Watering through the year

Water deeply, then let the mix dry out completely before watering again — typically every two to three weeks in spring and summer, and once a month or less in winter.

Water at soil level, never into the rosette. Water trapped between the tightly packed leaves causes rot at the crown, which is the fastest way to lose a haworthia. Bottom-watering works well for this species.

The leaves are the gauge. Plump and firm means well watered; slightly soft with a faint wrinkle means it is ready. Translucent, squashy leaves mean serious overwatering and rot is probably already underway at the roots. Humidity should be low; these plants dislike damp, stagnant air.

Soil and potting mix

Use a gritty cactus and succulent mix, or one part potting compost to one part coarse sand or pumice with added perlite. Fast drainage is the entire requirement.

Choose a small, shallow pot — haworthia roots are fine and relatively sparse, and an oversized pot holds wet soil the plant cannot use. Terracotta is ideal. A layer of grit on the surface keeps the lower leaves dry. Feed at most once or twice during the growing season with a half-strength succulent feed, and not at all in winter.

How to Propagate Zebra Haworthia

Zebra haworthia propagates by offsets, which is both the easiest and the only reliable method. Unlike echeveria, haworthia leaves do not root well — a detached leaf usually just shrivels — so do not waste time on leaf propagation.

Mature plants produce pups around the base of the rosette, often several at a time. Wait until an offset is at least an inch across and has begun forming its own roots. In spring, unpot the parent, brush the soil away and locate where the pup joins. Separate it with a clean knife, taking as much root as you can. Let the cut surfaces dry and callus for two to three days in a shaded spot before potting — skipping this step is the usual cause of failure.

Pot each offset into dry gritty mix in a small pot and wait a week before the first light watering. Keep them in bright indirect light rather than sun while they establish. Growth is slow but steady, and a pup typically reaches parent size in two to three years.

Common Zebra Haworthia Problems & Fixes

Leaves turning red, brown or bleached white

Usually means: Too much direct sun. Haworthia is a shade-dwelling succulent and burns more readily than most.

What to do: Move to bright indirect light, or filter the window with a sheer curtain. Mild colouring recovers over weeks; bleached tissue does not.

Rosette stretching upward, leaves pointing up and spaced out

Usually means: Insufficient light. The plant is etiolating.

What to do: Move somewhere brighter but still out of harsh sun. Stretched growth is permanent, though new growth from the centre comes in compact.

Leaves soft, translucent and mushy

Usually means: Overwatering. The leaves have taken up more water than they can hold and the cells are failing.

What to do: Stop watering and let the mix dry completely. Check the roots and the base of the rosette for rot, cut away soft tissue, and repot into dry gritty mix if needed.

Leaves thin, wrinkled and curling inward

Usually means: Underwatering over an extended period.

What to do: Water thoroughly until it drains, then again a week later. Leaves plump up over a week or two.

Brown mushy centre of the rosette

Usually means: Crown rot from water sitting between the leaves.

What to do: Usually fatal once the growing point goes. Salvage any healthy offsets immediately. Water at soil level only, or bottom-water.

Lower leaves drying and browning

Usually means: Normal ageing if it is only the oldest outer leaves; underwatering or root loss if widespread.

What to do: Gently pull away dried leaves. If many leaves are affecting, check the roots — root mealybugs are a common hidden cause in this genus.

White cottony spots at the leaf bases or among the roots

Usually means: Mealybugs, including root mealybugs which are easy to miss.

What to do: Dab foliage clusters with rubbing alcohol. For root mealybugs, unpot, wash the roots clean, and repot into fresh mix in a sterilised pot.

Repotting Zebra Haworthia

Haworthias are slow and like being snug, so repot every two to three years, or sooner if offsets have filled the pot.

Repot in spring into a small, shallow pot only slightly larger, in terracotta if possible. Use completely dry mix and do not water for a week afterwards so any damaged roots seal.

This is the natural moment to separate offsets and to inspect the roots for root mealybugs, which affect this genus disproportionately and are invisible until you unpot. Set the plant at the same depth — burying the lower leaves causes rot. Skip fertiliser for a couple of months.

Zebra Haworthia Varieties to Look For

Haworthiopsis fasciata and Haworthiopsis attenuata are both sold as zebra plant and are hard to tell apart; attenuata has white tubercles on both leaf surfaces while fasciata has them only underneath. Both are grown identically.

Haworthia cooperi is a completely different look — plump translucent leaves with windowed tips that let light into the plant body. Haworthia truncata, the horse's teeth, has flat-topped leaves in a row. Note the genus was split, so you may see these as Haworthia, Haworthiopsis or Tulista. All are non-toxic to cats and dogs, making them one of the safest succulent choices for a pet household.
