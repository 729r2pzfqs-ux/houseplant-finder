Lithops Care in Depth

Light

Lithops need more direct sun than almost any other houseplant — four to six hours daily, on the sunniest windowsill you have. A south-facing window is really the only position that works, and even then a grow light may be needed through winter in northern latitudes.

Insufficient light is the most common reason lithops fail. They respond by elongating upward, losing the flat pebble shape that defines them, and the stretched growth is permanent. In good light they stay squat and flush against the grit, with well-defined patterning on the top window. Introduce strong sun gradually to a plant bought from a dim shop, since sudden exposure scorches them.

Watering through the year

This is where lithops differ from every other plant you own, and where almost all of them are killed. They follow a strict annual cycle and watering at the wrong point in it is fatal.

From spring through summer, water very sparingly — a small amount every three to four weeks at most, and nothing at all during the hottest part of summer when the plant is dormant. In autumn, when the flower appears, water lightly every two to three weeks. Then stop completely from midwinter through spring while the new pair of leaves forms.

The critical rule: do not water at all while the old leaves are shrivelling and the new pair is emerging from between them, usually late winter into spring. The new leaves draw their water entirely from the old ones, and watering during this period causes the plant to split, burst or rot. This single mistake accounts for most lithops deaths.

Soil and potting mix

Use an extremely gritty mineral mix — at least half and preferably more coarse sand, pumice, grit or perlite, with only a small proportion of potting compost. Standard cactus compost is usually too rich and holds too much water for lithops.

Use a deep, narrow pot. Lithops have a surprisingly long taproot relative to the tiny body above ground, and a shallow pot cramps it. Top-dress with coarse grit so the body sits against mineral rather than damp compost. Do not feed; lithops need essentially no fertiliser and feeding causes them to bloat and split.

How to Propagate Lithops

Lithops are propagated from seed or, less commonly, by division of a clump. Leaf cuttings do not work — a single leaf from the pair will not root, since the growing point sits between them.

Seed is the standard method. Sow on the surface of damp gritty mix in spring, do not cover, and keep in a covered container in bright light at around 20°C. Germination takes one to three weeks. Seedlings are tiny and must be kept lightly moist for the first few months — the opposite of adult care — then gradually weaned onto the adult watering cycle over their first year. They take three to five years to reach flowering size, so this is a project measured in years.

Division applies to established clumps, which form as a plant produces multiple heads over time. In spring, before the new growth cycle, unpot and cut a head away with a portion of the taproot. Let the cut callus for a week before potting into dry gritty mix, and do not water for several weeks.

Common Lithops Problems & Fixes

Plant splits open or bursts

Usually means: Watering during the leaf-renewal period, or overwatering generally. The body takes up more water than it can contain.

What to do: Stop watering entirely and let it dry out. Damage is permanent for that pair of leaves but the plant often survives to produce a normal new pair next cycle. Learn the cycle and water accordingly.

Stretching upward and losing the flat shape

Usually means: Insufficient light — etiolation.

What to do: Move to the sunniest position available or add a grow light. The stretched body will not shorten, but the next pair of leaves will come in compact if the light is right.

Old leaves not shrivelling away as new ones emerge

Usually means: Watering during the renewal period, which keeps the old leaves plump instead of letting them be absorbed.

What to do: Stop watering completely. Let the old pair dry out naturally — it should become papery and can eventually be peeled away. Do not pull it off while still fleshy.

Body soft, mushy or collapsing

Usually means: Rot from overwatering, usually terminal.

What to do: Unpot and check the taproot. If any firm tissue remains, cut away the rot, let it dry for a week, and repot in dry mineral mix. Prevention is the only real answer with this species.

Wrinkled, shrunken body outside the renewal period

Usually means: Genuine underwatering, which is rare but does happen during the autumn growth period.

What to do: Water lightly and check again in a week. Make sure you are not confusing this with normal shrivelling during leaf renewal, when wrinkling is expected and correct.

More than two leaves per head

Usually means: Over-watering or over-feeding, which causes the plant to produce extra leaf pairs and lose its characteristic form.

What to do: Stop feeding entirely and cut watering right back. The plant will usually return to a normal single pair over subsequent cycles.

Brown scorched patch on the top surface

Usually means: Sunburn after a sudden move into intense sun, particularly on a plant recently bought from a poorly lit shop.

What to do: Increase sun exposure over two to three weeks rather than at once. Damaged tissue does not heal but is replaced at the next leaf renewal.

Repotting Lithops

Lithops need repotting rarely — every three to five years, or when a clump has outgrown its pot. They dislike disturbance and have a long fragile taproot that is easily damaged.

Repot in spring after the new leaf pair has fully emerged and the old one has dried away, which is the point of least stress. Choose a deep, narrow pot with excellent drainage.

Handle the taproot carefully and set the plant so the body sits just above the surface with the grit top-dressing around it, not buried. Do not water for two to three weeks afterwards. Never repot during the leaf-renewal period.

Lithops Varieties to Look For

There are dozens of Lithops species, distinguished by the patterning on the top window. Lithops lesliei has brown-grey tops with fine markings, L. olivacea is olive green with translucent windows, L. karasmontana has rust-coloured ridged patterning, and L. optica 'Rubra' is an unusual purple-pink.

Related living stones worth knowing are Conophytum, Pleiospilos nelii the split rock, and Fenestraria the baby toes — all with similar cycle-driven watering. Lithops are non-toxic to cats and dogs. The real hazard is to the plant rather than from it: they are frequently sold in ordinary compost in shallow pots, and repotting into a proper gritty mineral mix soon after purchase substantially improves their chances.
