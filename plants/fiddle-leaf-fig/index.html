About the Fiddle Leaf Fig

The Instagram-famous plant with large violin-shaped leaves. Beautiful but demanding.

Origin: West Africa

Care Tips

Bright indirect light, consistent watering, hates change.

Fiddle Leaf Fig Care in Depth

Light

Fiddle leaf fig needs more light than most people give it, and this is the root of its difficult reputation. It wants bright indirect light for most of the day — directly in front of an east window, or a few feet from a south or west window. In a dim corner it drops leaves steadily and never recovers properly.

A few hours of gentle direct morning sun is beneficial; harsh afternoon sun through unshaded glass will scorch the large leaves. Rotate the pot a quarter turn every week or two, because the plant leans hard toward its light source and develops a one-sided crown if left alone.

Watering through the year

Water when the top two inches of mix are dry, then water thoroughly until it runs from the drainage holes. In a bright spot during spring and summer that is usually every seven to ten days. This plant hates both extremes — it will drop leaves from drought and rot from constant wet.

Cut back substantially in autumn and winter, often to every two to three weeks. The combination of low winter light and unchanged watering is what kills most fiddle leaf figs in their first year.

Consistency matters more than frequency here. Fiddle leaf figs respond badly to erratic care, so pick a checking routine and stick to it. They prefer humidity above 40% and dislike cold draughts intensely — keep them away from doors, single-glazed windows and air conditioning vents.

Soil and potting mix

Use a well-draining potting mix with plenty of structure: roughly two parts quality potting compost to one part perlite and one part orchid bark. Ficus lyrata roots need air, and a dense peat-heavy mix that stays soggy is a direct route to root rot.

The pot must drain freely. Feed every four weeks through spring and summer with a balanced liquid fertiliser at half strength, or use a fertiliser formulated for foliage plants with a higher nitrogen ratio. Stop feeding entirely from October to March.

How to Propagate Fiddle Leaf Fig

Fiddle leaf figs propagate from stem cuttings taken in spring or early summer. Choose a section of stem six to twelve inches long with one or two healthy leaves, and cut just below a node with clean, sharp secateurs. The plant bleeds a milky white latex when cut — it irritates skin and is toxic if ingested, so wear gloves and rinse the cut end before proceeding.

Stand the cutting in water in a bright spot out of direct sun, changing the water weekly, or pot it straight into damp, free-draining mix. Rooting is slower than for most houseplants, typically four to eight weeks, and warmth makes a real difference. A clear bag loosely over a soil-potted cutting raises humidity and improves the odds. Once roots are two to three inches long, pot into your usual mix and keep it slightly moister than an established plant for the first few weeks. Air layering is the more reliable method for a tall, bare-stemmed plant, and has the advantage of producing a large plant immediately rather than a single-stem cutting.

Common Fiddle Leaf Fig Problems & Fixes

Brown spots in the middle of leaves with dark edges

Usually means: Root rot from overwatering. On fiddle leaf figs the damage often shows in the leaf centre rather than the edges, and spreads outward.

What to do: Check the roots. Trim anything brown and soft, repot into fresh free-draining mix, and reduce watering. Affected leaves will not recover and can be removed.

Brown, dry patches starting at the leaf edges

Usually means: Underwatering or very low humidity, sometimes combined with a plant sitting near a radiator.

What to do: Water more consistently and move it away from heat sources. Raising humidity to 40-50% helps noticeably.

Sudden leaf drop

Usually means: Stress from change — a new position, a cold draught, a sharp temperature swing, or an abrupt shift in watering. Fiddle leaf figs are unusually sensitive to being moved.

What to do: Settle it in one spot with stable conditions and stop adjusting things. Some drop after a move home is normal; give it a month to acclimatise before intervening further.

Leaves pale, small, and widely spaced up a leggy stem

Usually means: Not enough light. The plant stretches toward the window and cannot support full-sized foliage.

What to do: Move it into genuinely bright indirect light. Prune the top back to encourage branching, and rotate the pot regularly so it grows evenly.

Red or brown speckling on new leaves

Usually means: Oedema — cells rupturing because the plant took up water faster than it could transpire it, usually after heavy watering in low light.

What to do: Water less and increase light. The spotting is cosmetic and does not spread; new leaves will come out clean once conditions are right.

White fuzzy spots or fine webbing on leaf undersides

Usually means: Mealybugs or spider mites, both of which shelter under the broad leaves where they go unnoticed.

What to do: Wipe every leaf on both sides with a damp cloth, then treat with insecticidal soap or neem oil weekly for three to four weeks. Inspect the undersides regularly — this is the spot people forget.

Dusty leaves looking dull

Usually means: Simple dust accumulation. The large flat leaves collect it quickly, and a heavy layer measurably reduces the light reaching the leaf.

What to do: Wipe both sides with a damp cloth every few weeks. This is genuine maintenance for this species rather than cosmetic.

Repotting Fiddle Leaf Fig

Repot every one to two years while young, then every two to three years once mature. Signs are roots circling the surface or emerging from the drainage holes, water running straight through, and growth stalling during the growing season.

Spring is the right time. Move up one pot size only — typically two inches wider. Fiddle leaf figs are top-heavy, so choose a substantial pot that will not tip; a wide, heavy ceramic container is safer than a tall narrow one.

Handle the root ball gently and disturb it as little as possible, because this species resents root disturbance and often sulks for a few weeks afterwards. Water thoroughly, keep it out of direct sun for a fortnight, and do not fertilise for six weeks. For very large specimens that cannot practically be repotted, top-dress instead: scrape off the top two inches of mix and replace with fresh compost each spring.

Fiddle Leaf Fig Varieties to Look For

'Bambino' is the dwarf cultivar, reaching three to four feet with smaller, rounder leaves — a far more practical choice for an average room than the species, which can reach ten feet indoors. 'Compacta' is similar, with tightly spaced leaves on a bushier frame.

'Variegata' carries cream and green marbling, is considerably harder to keep and needs bright light to avoid the white sections browning. Whichever form you choose, Ficus lyrata is toxic to cats and dogs, and the milky sap irritates skin on contact — worth knowing before you prune.
