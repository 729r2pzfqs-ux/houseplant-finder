About the Lemon Button Fern

Compact fern with small round leaflets. Smells lemony when brushed.

Origin: Worldwide tropics

Care Tips

Low to medium light, keep moist, more forgiving than most ferns.

Lemon Button Fern Care in Depth

Light

Lemon button fern wants medium indirect light and tolerates lower light well. It is a compact relative of the Boston fern and shares its preference for the dappled shade of a forest floor.

A north or east window suits it well, or a position back from a brighter one. Direct sun scorches the small round leaflets and bleaches them yellow. In deep shade it survives but grows sparsely. Its small size makes it easy to place somewhere with gentle light, which is part of why it succeeds where a full-sized Boston fern struggles.

Watering through the year

Keep the mix consistently moist but not soggy, watering when the top half-inch feels dry — usually every four to six days in spring and summer, and every seven to ten in winter.

Lemon button fern is noticeably more drought-tolerant than Boston or maidenhair fern, which is its main practical advantage. It will not collapse the moment it dries out, though a fully dried root ball still causes browning that does not reverse.

Humidity above 50% is preferred and the plant tolerates ordinary household air considerably better than other Nephrolepis. Dry air shows as browning frond tips. A pebble tray helps, and a bathroom or kitchen position suits it well. It is sensitive to salts, so flush the pot occasionally.

Soil and potting mix

Use a peat or coir-based potting mix with added perlite — something that holds moisture while still draining. The fibrous root system wants consistent dampness without waterlogging.

Plastic or glazed pots conserve moisture better than terracotta. Feed monthly at quarter to half strength through spring and summer only. Ferns are sensitive to fertiliser salts, and excess shows as browned frond tips. Flush the pot with plain water every couple of months.

How to Propagate Lemon Button Fern

Lemon button fern is propagated by division, which is straightforward and the practical home method.

Divide in spring. Slide the plant out of its pot and look at the dense fibrous root mass. Mature plants form multiple crowns, and these pull apart into separate plants. Use a clean serrated knife if the root ball is too dense to separate by hand.

Each division needs several fronds and a healthy share of roots. Pot each into fresh moisture-retentive mix at the same depth, water thoroughly and keep somewhere humid and shaded for two to three weeks while they recover. Expect some frond loss after division; new growth follows once the roots re-establish. Like other Nephrolepis, this fern also produces thin wiry runners that root where they touch damp soil, so pinning one down into an adjacent pot is an easy alternative.

Common Lemon Button Fern Problems & Fixes

Brown, crispy frond tips

Usually means: Dry air, underwatering, or fertiliser salt buildup.

What to do: Raise humidity, keep the soil evenly moist, and flush the pot with plain water every couple of months. Trim damaged tips.

Fronds dropping leaflets

Usually means: Low humidity or the soil drying out — though far less severe than in a Boston fern.

What to do: Raise humidity above 50%, move away from radiators, and water more consistently. Some shedding of old fronds is normal.

Whole fronds yellowing and dying back

Usually means: Overwatering if the mix is sodden, or severe drought if the root ball has shrunk from the pot sides.

What to do: Feel the soil. If soggy, improve drainage and water less. If bone dry with water running down the edge gap, submerge the pot for twenty minutes to rehydrate.

Pale or bleached fronds

Usually means: Too much light, usually direct sun.

What to do: Move to a shadier position with indirect light only. Bleached fronds do not recover; trim them out.

Sparse, thin growth

Usually means: Too little light, an exhausted mix, or a plant that needs dividing after several years.

What to do: Move somewhere with slightly more indirect light, repot into fresh mix in spring, and divide if the clump is congested.

Brown dots in rows on frond undersides

Usually means: Spores, which are normal on a mature fern.

What to do: No action needed. Irregularly scattered bumps that scrape off are scale insects instead.

Fine webbing between fronds

Usually means: Spider mites in dry air.

What to do: Rinse thoroughly in the shower and treat with insecticidal soap weekly for three weeks — test on one frond first, since ferns are sensitive to soaps and neem.

Repotting Lemon Button Fern

Repot every one to two years in spring, or when roots have filled the pot and the plant dries out within a day of watering.

Move up one size, choosing plastic or glazed ceramic to conserve moisture, and make sure it drains. The root system is shallow and fibrous, so a wide pot suits it better than a deep one.

Loosen the root mass gently and trim dead roots. This is the natural moment to divide a crowded plant. Water well, keep humidity up for a few weeks, and do not feed for six weeks.

Lemon Button Fern Varieties to Look For

Nephrolepis cordifolia 'Duffii' is the lemon button fern, a compact form with small round leaflets along arching fronds, reaching about twelve inches. The name comes from a faint lemon scent released when the fronds are crushed.

Within the genus, Nephrolepis exaltata 'Bostoniensis' is the classic Boston fern, much larger and far more demanding about humidity, while 'Dallas' and 'Compacta' sit between the two. Lemon button fern is the most practical Nephrolepis for an ordinary room, being both smaller and considerably more tolerant of dry air. All Nephrolepis are non-toxic to cats and dogs.
