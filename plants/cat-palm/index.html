About the Cat Palm

Full, bushy palm with soft fronds. Pet safe but needs moisture.

Origin: Mexico

Care Tips

Medium to bright light, keep consistently moist, needs humidity.

Cat Palm Care in Depth

Light

Cat palm wants bright indirect light and tolerates medium light better than areca or majesty palm, though not as well as parlor or kentia. A position in front of an east window, or a few feet back from a south or west window, suits it well.

Direct sun yellows and scorches the fronds — this is one of the more sun-sensitive palms. In light that is too low the plant thins out and produces few new fronds. Rotate the pot every few weeks so the clump grows evenly rather than leaning toward the window.

Watering through the year

Keep the mix evenly moist through spring and summer, watering when the top inch is dry — usually every five to seven days. Cat palm comes from stream banks in southern Mexico and is one of the thirstier palms, disliking drying out.

Reduce to every ten to fourteen days in winter but never let the root ball dry through. Fronds that brown from drought are lost permanently, since palms do not regreen damaged tissue.

Humidity above 50% matters considerably. Dry indoor air produces brown frond tips and invites spider mites, which are the most common cat palm problem. It is also sensitive to fluoride and salts, so filtered or rainwater makes a visible difference to tip browning.

Soil and potting mix

Use a well-draining potting mix with added perlite, or a palm-specific compost. The mix should hold moisture while draining freely — cat palm wants consistency, not saturation.

The pot must drain, and a heavy container suits a clump that becomes top-heavy. Feed monthly at half strength through spring and summer with a palm fertiliser containing micronutrients, since this genus is prone to magnesium and potassium deficiency. Stop in winter and flush the pot with clean water a few times a year.

How to Propagate Cat Palm

Cat palm is propagated by division, not by cuttings. Like all palms it grows from a terminal point on each cane, so a cut stem will not regrow and a severed frond will not root.

What makes division possible is that cat palm is genuinely clumping — it produces new canes from the base, forming a multi-stemmed clump with a shared but separable root mass. This distinguishes it from parlor palm, which is usually just several seedlings potted together.

Divide in spring. Slide the plant out and look for groups of canes with their own roots. The root mass is dense, so a clean serrated knife is usually needed. Keep at least three or four canes per division so each still looks substantial. Pot into fresh palm mix, water thoroughly, and keep warm, humid and out of direct sun for a month. Expect a setback with some frond browning before growth resumes — cat palm resents root disturbance.

Common Cat Palm Problems & Fixes

Brown frond tips

Usually means: Dry air, fluoride and salts in tap water, or inconsistent watering — the most common cat palm complaint.

What to do: Raise humidity above 50%, switch to filtered or rainwater, and flush the pot with clean water every couple of months. Trim the brown tip following the natural taper, leaving a sliver of dead tissue.

Fine webbing, stippled and dusty-looking fronds

Usually means: Spider mites, which thrive in dry indoor air.

What to do: Rinse the entire plant in the shower including frond undersides, then treat with insecticidal soap every five days for three cycles. Raising humidity prevents recurrence.

Whole fronds browning from the bottom of the plant

Usually means: The root ball dried out, or natural shedding of the oldest fronds.

What to do: Check whether water runs down the gap at the pot edge rather than soaking in; if so, submerge the pot to rehydrate. Cut dead fronds at the base.

Yellowing fronds with spotting

Usually means: Magnesium or potassium deficiency, which palms are prone to.

What to do: Feed with a palm fertiliser containing micronutrients through the growing season and repot into fresh mix if the plant has been in the same soil for years.

Pale or scorched fronds

Usually means: Direct sun, which this palm tolerates less well than most.

What to do: Move out of direct light into bright filtered conditions.

No new fronds for months

Usually means: Insufficient light, cold, or an exhausted mix.

What to do: Move brighter, keep above 18°C, feed in the growing season, and repot every two to three years.

Canes soft and browning at soil level

Usually means: Root rot from waterlogged soil or a pot standing in water.

What to do: Confirm drainage and empty the saucer. Cut affected canes at the base and repot into fresh mix.

Repotting Cat Palm

Repot every two to three years in spring. Cat palm fills a pot with dense roots and exhausts its mix, so refreshing the medium matters as much as extra space.

Move up one pot size into a heavy, stable container. Palm roots are brittle and resent disturbance, so handle the root ball gently and avoid teasing it apart unless deliberately dividing.

Keep the clump of canes together — the full appearance depends on them growing as one. Water thoroughly, keep humidity high, and expect some frond browning as a stress response. Do not feed for six weeks.

Cat Palm Varieties to Look For

Chamaedorea cataractarum is the cat palm, a clumping species with dense arching fronds and no visible trunk, staying around three to six feet indoors. There are no meaningful cultivars.

Related species include Chamaedorea elegans, the parlor palm, which is far more shade-tolerant, and C. seifrizii, the bamboo palm, which is taller with visible canes. Cat palm is bushier and fuller at the base than either. All Chamaedorea are non-toxic to cats and dogs, which along with their moderate size makes them practical choices for pet households — though cat palm's humidity needs make it less forgiving than parlor palm.
