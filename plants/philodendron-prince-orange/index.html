Philodendron Prince of Orange Care in Depth

Light

Philodendron Prince of Orange wants bright indirect light. The colour is the whole point of this plant — new leaves emerge bright copper-orange and mature through amber to green — and that transition is far more vivid in good light.

An east window is ideal, or near a south or west window behind a sheer curtain. In medium or low light new leaves emerge a muted yellow-green rather than orange, and the plant loses the quality that distinguishes it. Direct sun scorches the leaves and bleaches the colour. Rotate the pot every few weeks since this is a self-heading philodendron that grows as a rosette and can become lopsided.

Watering through the year

Water when the top inch of mix is dry, then water thoroughly and drain fully. In spring and summer that is usually every seven to ten days; in winter, every two weeks.

Prince of Orange is a self-heading philodendron with a compact root system and is reasonably prone to root rot, so let the surface dry properly rather than keeping it damp. Yellowing lower leaves with wet soil is the early warning.

Humidity above 50% produces better growth and larger leaves, and dry air shows as brown crispy edges. A pebble tray or humidifier helps. Empty the saucer after watering; standing water is the usual route to rot in this genus.

Soil and potting mix

Use a chunky aroid mix — roughly equal parts potting compost, orchid bark and perlite. The roots want air as well as moisture, and dense peat-based compost that stays wet is the usual cause of trouble.

Keep the pot modest in size; this is a compact grower and an oversized pot holds wet mix around a small root system. Drainage holes are essential. Feed monthly at half strength through spring and summer to support the colourful new growth, and stop in winter.

How to Propagate Philodendron Prince of Orange

Prince of Orange is a self-heading philodendron rather than a vining one, which changes how propagation works — there are no long trailing stems full of nodes to cut.

The practical methods are division and stem cuttings from a mature plant. Over time the plant develops a short thick stem with visible nodes and often produces basal offshoots. If your plant has pups at the base, unpot in spring, separate an offshoot with its own roots using a clean knife, and pot into fresh aroid mix.

For a taller plant with an exposed stem, cut a section containing at least one node and an aerial root, and root it in damp sphagnum moss or perlite under high humidity. This is slower than vining philodendrons — expect four to eight weeks. Keep the humidity high while rooting and the temperature warm, and pot on once roots are two inches long.

Common Philodendron Prince of Orange Problems & Fixes

New leaves emerging pale yellow-green rather than orange

Usually means: Insufficient light. The orange pigmentation in new growth is a light response.

What to do: Move to brighter indirect light. Existing leaves keep the colour they emerged with, but new growth will come in vivid.

New leaves turning green quickly

Usually means: Normal. The orange colour is only on new growth and matures to green over several weeks — this is the plant's natural cycle, not a fault.

What to do: No action needed. A well-grown plant produces new orange leaves regularly, so there is always some colour.

Yellowing lower leaves with damp soil

Usually means: Overwatering and the start of root rot.

What to do: Let the top inch dry properly between waterings and confirm the pot drains. Repot into a chunkier aroid mix if the medium stays soggy.

Brown crispy leaf edges

Usually means: Low humidity or salt buildup from fertiliser and hard water.

What to do: Raise humidity above 50% and flush the pot with clean water every couple of months.

Plant leaning or growing lopsided

Usually means: Growing toward a single light source without being rotated.

What to do: Rotate a quarter turn every few weeks. Self-heading philodendrons show a lean more obviously than vining ones.

Leaves drooping though the soil is wet

Usually means: Root rot preventing water uptake.

What to do: Unpot, cut away soft brown roots, and repot into fresh airy mix in a smaller pot. Water sparingly until new growth appears.

White cottony clusters in the leaf axils

Usually means: Mealybugs, which favour the tight crown of a self-heading philodendron.

What to do: Dab each cluster with rubbing alcohol on a cotton bud, working into the crown. Check weekly for a month.

Repotting Philodendron Prince of Orange

Prince of Orange grows at a moderate pace and needs repotting every two years, or when roots have genuinely filled the pot.

Move up one small size only into fresh chunky aroid mix. Overpotting leads directly to root rot given the compact root system.

Repot in spring, handling the crown carefully since the growing point sits at the centre. Water lightly afterwards, keep humidity up for a few weeks, and wait six weeks before feeding. Separate any basal offsets at this point if you want more plants.

Philodendron Prince of Orange Varieties to Look For

Philodendron 'Prince of Orange' is a self-heading hybrid whose new leaves emerge bright copper-orange, passing through amber and salmon before maturing to green. The colour range on a healthy plant at any one time is its main appeal.

Related self-heading philodendrons include 'Rojo Congo', with deep burgundy new growth, 'Birkin' with white pinstripes, 'Moonlight' in bright chartreuse, and 'Imperial Red'. All are compact rosette-forming plants rather than vines. Every philodendron is toxic to cats and dogs due to calcium oxalate crystals, causing mouth irritation and drooling if chewed.
