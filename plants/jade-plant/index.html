About the Jade Plant

Thick woody stems and oval leaves. Can live for decades with proper care.

Origin: South Africa

Care Tips

Bright light, water when completely dry, well-draining soil.

Jade Plant Care in Depth

Light

Jade plant needs a lot of light — four or more hours of direct sun daily is ideal. A south or west-facing windowsill is the right home. This is the single biggest factor in whether a jade develops the thick, compact, tree-like form people want or a floppy, stretched one.

In insufficient light the stems elongate, the leaves space out and thin, and the plant eventually leans and topples under its own weight. Good light also brings out the red edging on the leaves, which is a healthy sun response rather than a warning. Introduce strong sun gradually to avoid scorching a plant that has been in dimmer conditions.

Watering through the year

Treat jade as the succulent it is. Water deeply, then let the mix dry out completely before watering again. In spring and summer that usually works out at every two to three weeks; in winter, once a month or less, especially in a cool room.

The leaves are the best gauge. Firm and plump means the plant is well watered; slightly soft and wrinkled means it is ready for a drink. Shrivelled, papery leaves mean it has been dry too long, and soft translucent ones mean it is far too wet.

Water the soil, not the foliage, and never leave the pot in a saucer of water. Humidity is irrelevant; jade is perfectly happy in dry indoor air and actually prefers it.

Soil and potting mix

Use a cactus or succulent mix, or two parts potting compost to one part coarse sand or perlite. The mix needs to drain fast and dry out fully between waterings.

Terracotta is strongly preferred. Jade grows top-heavy as it matures, and a heavy pot prevents tipping while the porous clay helps the root ball dry. Pick a pot that is wide rather than deep, since the roots are relatively shallow. Feed only two or three times across spring and summer with a half-strength succulent fertiliser, and not at all in winter.

How to Propagate Jade Plant

Jade is among the most rewarding plants to propagate because both leaves and stem cuttings work, and the success rate is high. The key step that people skip is callusing.

For leaf propagation, gently twist a plump healthy leaf off the stem so it comes away cleanly with its base intact — a torn leaf will not root. For stem cuttings, take a three to four inch section with several leaves. Either way, leave the cutting on a dry surface out of direct sun for three to seven days until the cut end has dried and sealed. Then lay leaves flat on dry cactus mix, or insert stem cuttings an inch deep. Do not water for a week, then mist lightly or water sparingly every few days. Roots form in two to three weeks and a tiny plantlet follows at the base of a leaf after a month or more. Bright indirect light and warmth speed things up; damp soil at the callusing stage is the one thing that reliably causes failure.

Common Jade Plant Problems & Fixes

Leaves soft, translucent and dropping at a touch

Usually means: Overwatering. Jade leaves become waterlogged and lose structure, and this is the most common way jades are killed.

What to do: Stop watering immediately and let the mix dry out completely. Check the roots and stem base for rot; cut away soft tissue and repot into dry gritty mix if found. Dropped leaves can be callused and rooted as insurance.

Leaves wrinkled, thin and papery

Usually means: Underwatering over an extended period.

What to do: Water thoroughly until it drains, then again a week later. Leaves plump back up over a week or two. Much easier to correct than overwatering.

Stems long and leggy, leaves widely spaced

Usually means: Not enough light. The plant is stretching, and the resulting growth is weak.

What to do: Move to the sunniest window available. Prune the stretched growth back to encourage branching — jade responds very well to pruning and the cuttings root easily.

Leaf edges turning red or bronze

Usually means: Sun stress, which in jade is normal and generally considered desirable. Occasionally it indicates underwatering.

What to do: No action needed if watering is adequate. If you prefer plain green foliage, move slightly out of the most direct sun.

Black or brown soft patches at the base of the stem

Usually means: Stem or root rot, usually from wet soil in cool conditions.

What to do: Take healthy cuttings from the upper stems immediately. Unpot, cut back to firm white tissue, let it dry for several days, and repot into dry mix. Advanced stem rot is often fatal, so the cuttings are your real insurance.

White cottony spots in the leaf joints

Usually means: Mealybugs, which favour the tight angles where leaves meet stems.

What to do: Dab each one with rubbing alcohol on a cotton bud, and inspect weekly for a month. Jade's compact growth gives them plenty of hiding places.

Sudden loss of many leaves at once

Usually means: A sharp change in conditions — a cold draught, a move, or a drastic swing in watering. Jade sheds leaves as a stress response.

What to do: Settle it in a stable bright position away from draughts and resume consistent care. New growth follows once conditions steady.

Repotting Jade Plant

Jade grows slowly and likes being snug, so repot only every three to four years. The prompts are roots filling the pot entirely, a plant that has become unstable and top-heavy, or mix that has broken down and no longer drains.

Repot in spring into a pot one size larger — wide, heavy and shallow rather than tall. Stability matters: a mature jade is genuinely heavy and a light plastic pot will tip.

Use dry mix, handle the brittle branches carefully, and do not water for a week afterwards so any damaged roots can seal. Skip fertiliser for a couple of months. If the plant has grown lopsided, repotting is also the moment to prune it back into shape; jade tolerates hard pruning well and branches from just below each cut.

Jade Plant Varieties to Look For

Crassula ovata is the standard jade, with rounded deep green leaves. 'Hobbit' and 'Gollum' have tubular, trumpet-shaped leaves and are popular for bonsai-style training. 'Tricolor' is variegated in cream, green and pink and needs strong light to hold the colour.

'Sunset' develops vivid red and yellow tones in bright light. Crassula arborescens, the silver jade, has blue-grey leaves with red margins and is a distinct species with the same care needs. All jade plants are toxic to cats and dogs, causing vomiting and lethargy if eaten — and because the leaves detach so easily, they are worth keeping well out of reach of a curious pet.
