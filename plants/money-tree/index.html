Money Tree Care in Depth

Light

Money tree wants bright indirect light and tolerates medium light, where growth slows considerably. In its native Central American wetlands it grows as an understorey tree, so filtered light rather than full sun is what it expects.

An east window, or a few feet back from a south or west window, suits it well. Direct afternoon sun scorches the leaflets. Rotate the pot every few weeks — money trees lean toward light quite strongly, and since most are sold as braided trunks with a formal shape, an uneven crown spoils the look. Sudden changes in light cause leaf drop, so move it gradually if relocating.

Watering through the year

Water thoroughly when the top two inches of mix are dry, letting water run from the drainage holes, then let it dry down again. In spring and summer that is usually every seven to ten days; in winter, every two to three weeks.

Money tree stores water in its swollen trunk base and is more tolerant of drought than of saturation. Overwatering is the main cause of failure and shows as yellowing leaves and a soft trunk.

Humidity above 50% is preferred and dry air shows as browning leaflet tips. A pebble tray helps. This plant is often sold in a decorative outer pot with no drainage — check, because water collecting invisibly at the bottom is the single most common reason money trees die.

Soil and potting mix

Use a well-draining potting mix with added perlite and a little sand — something closer to a cactus blend than a moisture-retentive compost. The trunk stores water, so the roots do not need the soil to.

Drainage holes are essential. Feed every four to six weeks at half strength through spring and summer, stopping in winter. Money trees are light feeders and over-fertilising causes leaf drop. A pot that is snug rather than roomy suits them; excess wet soil around the roots is the main risk.

How to Propagate Money Tree

Money tree propagates from stem cuttings, though with lower success rates than most houseplants and with one important caveat about appearance.

Take a cutting six inches long with several leaves, from a healthy stem, cutting just below a node in spring or early summer. Remove the lower leaves, dip the cut end in rooting hormone, and insert into damp free-draining mix. Cover with a clear bag to maintain humidity and keep warm in bright indirect light. Roots take four to eight weeks and not every cutting succeeds.

The caveat: a cutting grows as a single straight stem and will never develop the swollen trunk base or the braided form of a shop-bought plant. Braiding is done by growers who plant several young seedlings together and weave the flexible green stems while they are still pliable, tightening the braid as they grow. You can braid your own if you have several young plants with green stems, but once the trunks lignify the shape is fixed. Seed propagation produces the characteristic swollen base but seed is rarely available.

Common Money Tree Problems & Fixes

Yellowing leaves dropping steadily

Usually means: Overwatering most commonly, particularly if the plant sits in a decorative pot with no drainage.

What to do: Check for standing water in the outer pot immediately. Let the top two inches dry before watering and make sure water can escape. This alone fixes most struggling money trees.

Sudden leaf drop after being moved

Usually means: Shock from a change in light or temperature. Money trees are sensitive to relocation.

What to do: Settle it in one position away from draughts and keep care consistent. Recovery usually takes a few weeks. Avoid making further changes while it adjusts.

Trunk soft or squashy

Usually means: Trunk rot from chronic overwatering. Serious, and often advanced by the time it is noticeable.

What to do: Unpot immediately, cut away soft tissue to firm material, let it dry for several days, and repot in dry free-draining mix. Take stem cuttings as insurance. If several braided trunks are affected, the others may still be saved.

Brown crispy leaflet tips

Usually means: Low humidity, underwatering, or salts in tap water.

What to do: Raise humidity, water more consistently, and flush the pot with plain water every few months.

Leggy stems with leaves only at the top

Usually means: Insufficient light combined with no pruning.

What to do: Increase light and prune the crown back in spring. Money tree branches readily from below a cut, which is how the rounded canopy is maintained.

One trunk in the braid dying while others are fine

Usually means: Normal in braided specimens — the trunks compete and the weakest sometimes fails. Also a sign of localised rot.

What to do: Cut the dead trunk out at the base. The remaining trunks continue and the braid usually still looks acceptable. Check drainage in case rot is the cause.

Fine webbing or cottony white spots

Usually means: Spider mites or mealybugs, both drawn to the dense canopy and dry air.

What to do: Rinse the plant, wipe leaflets on both sides, and treat with insecticidal soap weekly for a month.

Repotting Money Tree

Repot every two to three years in spring. Money trees are moderate growers and prefer being slightly snug, so there is no benefit to frequent repotting.

Move up one pot size only into free-draining mix. A pot that is too large holds wet soil around the roots, which is the main hazard for this species. Make sure the new pot drains, and if you use a decorative outer pot, either drill it or remove the inner pot to water.

Handle the braided trunks carefully and set the plant at the same depth. Water thoroughly and wait six weeks before feeding. Expect some leaf drop after repotting as a stress response.

Money Tree Varieties to Look For

Pachira aquatica is the species, sold as money tree or Malabar chestnut. What varies commercially is the form: single-trunk plants, braided multi-stem specimens, and small bonsai-style plants with a pronounced swollen base.

A variegated form with cream-splashed leaflets exists and needs brighter light. The plant is closely associated with feng shui, where the five leaflets per stem and the braided trunk are said to be auspicious, which is why it is so commonly given as a gift. Pachira aquatica is non-toxic to cats and dogs, which together with its sculptural form makes it one of the better large statement plants for a pet household.
