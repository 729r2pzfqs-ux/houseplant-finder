About the Jelly Bean Plant

Plump jelly bean shaped leaves that turn red in bright light.

Origin: Mexico

Care Tips

Full sun for best color, water when completely dry.

Jelly Bean Plant Care in Depth

Light

Jelly bean plant needs bright light with several hours of direct sun to develop the red tips it is grown for. In good light the bean-shaped leaves flush deep red at the ends; in shade they stay plain green and the plant looks unremarkable.

A south or west-facing windowsill is the right position. In insufficient light the stems stretch, the leaves space out, and the plant becomes floppy and sprawling. Introduce strong sun gradually — the leaves carry a light farina coating and a plant moved abruptly from shade will scorch. The red colouring is a stress response to sun and is entirely healthy.

Watering through the year

This is a true succulent with plump water-storing leaves. Water thoroughly, then let the mix dry out completely before watering again — typically every two to three weeks in spring and summer, and once a month or less in winter.

The leaves are the gauge: plump and firm means well watered, slightly soft and wrinkled means it is time. Mushy translucent leaves that drop at a touch mean serious overwatering.

Water at soil level and avoid letting water sit against the stems. Humidity should be low. Like burro's tail, this plant sheds leaves readily when handled, and each dropped leaf will happily root, so expect volunteer seedlings around the pot.

Soil and potting mix

Use a gritty cactus and succulent mix, or two parts succulent compost to one part coarse sand or perlite. Fast drainage is essential.

Terracotta is preferred, in a pot only slightly wider than the plant. A layer of grit on the surface keeps the lower leaves dry. Feed at most twice during the growing season with a half-strength succulent feed, and never in winter. Over-feeding produces soft green growth at the expense of the red colouring.

How to Propagate Jelly Bean Plant

Jelly bean plant is among the easiest succulents to propagate, and like burro's tail it does much of the work itself — dropped leaves root wherever they land.

For leaf propagation, gently twist a plump leaf off so it comes away cleanly with its base intact, or simply collect the ones that fall. Lay them on the surface of dry cactus mix and leave them alone for several days to callus. Then mist lightly every few days. Roots and a tiny rosette form at the base within two to four weeks, and the parent leaf shrivels as the baby draws on it.

Stem cuttings root just as easily. Take a section three to four inches long, strip the lowest leaves, and let it callus for two to three days before inserting into dry mix. Keep in bright indirect light while rooting, then move into sun gradually. Note that the sap can irritate skin, so wash your hands after taking a lot of cuttings.

Common Jelly Bean Plant Problems & Fixes

Leaves staying plain green with no red tips

Usually means: Insufficient light. The red colouring is a sun response.

What to do: Move to the sunniest window available, increasing exposure gradually. The colour develops over several weeks of good light.

Leaves dropping at the slightest touch

Usually means: Normal for this species, though excessive dropping can indicate over- or underwatering.

What to do: Site the plant where it will not be brushed past and handle it by the pot. Collect the fallen leaves and propagate them.

Leaves mushy and translucent

Usually means: Overwatering.

What to do: Stop watering and let the mix dry completely. Remove mushy leaves so rot does not spread, and check the stems and roots for soft tissue.

Leaves wrinkled and shrunken

Usually means: Underwatering over an extended period.

What to do: Water thoroughly and repeat in a week. Leaves plump back up within days.

Stems stretching with widely spaced leaves

Usually means: Insufficient light — etiolation.

What to do: Move to full sun gradually. Stretched growth is permanent; cut the affected stems back and propagate the cuttings, which will grow compact in better light.

Brown scorched patches on the leaves

Usually means: Sunburn after an abrupt move into intense sun.

What to do: Increase exposure over two to three weeks rather than at once. Note that red colouring is healthy sun stress, while brown papery patches are genuine damage.

Skin irritation after handling

Usually means: The sap of Sedum rubrotinctum irritates skin in some people.

What to do: Wash hands after taking cuttings or repotting, and wear gloves if you are sensitive.

Repotting Jelly Bean Plant

Jelly bean plant has a small root system and is happy snug, so repot only every two to three years, or when the mix has broken down.

Repot in spring into a slightly larger terracotta pot with gritty mix. Expect to lose a good number of leaves in the process — collect them for propagation.

Use dry mix and wait a week before watering so damaged roots seal. Skip fertiliser for a couple of months. Because the plant sheds so readily, many growers simply refresh the top layer of grit instead of repotting fully.

Jelly Bean Plant Varieties to Look For

Sedum rubrotinctum is the jelly bean plant or pork and beans, with plump cylindrical leaves that flush red at the tips in sun. 'Aurora' is a paler cultivar whose leaves turn pink and cream rather than green and red, and is slower-growing.

Related sedums include Sedum morganianum, the burro's tail, with long trailing stems, and Sedum nussbaumerianum, the coppertone stonecrop, with orange-toned leaves. Note the safety difference within the genus: while most Sedum species are listed as non-toxic, Sedum rubrotinctum is reported to be mildly toxic to cats and dogs, causing vomiting, and its sap irritates skin. Worth siting out of reach.
