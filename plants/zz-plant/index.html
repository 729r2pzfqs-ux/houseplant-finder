About the ZZ Plant

Virtually indestructible with waxy, dark green leaves. Thrives on neglect.

Origin: East Africa

Care Tips

Any light including low, water monthly, very drought tolerant.

ZZ Plant Care in Depth

Light

ZZ plant tolerates lower light than almost anything else sold as a houseplant, including windowless offices lit only by fluorescent tubes. It genuinely survives there, thanks to the potato-like rhizomes under the soil that store water and energy.

That said, it grows faster and more upright in bright indirect light. In very low light expect perhaps one or two new stems a year, and those stems may lean toward the light source. Direct afternoon sun scorches the glossy leaflets, so filtered light is the target if you want actual growth rather than mere survival.

Watering through the year

This is the most drought-tolerant common houseplant, and almost every ZZ that dies is drowned rather than starved. The rhizomes hold weeks of water in reserve. Let the mix dry out completely — all the way through, not just the top inch — before watering again.

In practice that means every two to three weeks in a bright spot during summer, and as little as once every four to six weeks in winter or in low light. Water thoroughly when you do, letting it run from the drainage holes, then leave it alone entirely.

If you are ever unsure, wait. A ZZ that has gone too long shows wrinkled stems and drooping leaflets and recovers completely from a good soak. A ZZ that has been kept wet develops rhizome rot, which is usually fatal by the time it shows above the soil. Humidity is irrelevant to this plant.

Soil and potting mix

Use a fast-draining mix — cactus and succulent compost, or ordinary potting mix cut with a third perlite or coarse sand. The rhizomes need air around them and rot in dense, water-retentive media.

Drainage holes are non-negotiable. Terracotta helps if you are a generous waterer. Feed very lightly: a balanced fertiliser at half strength two or three times across spring and summer is plenty, and none in winter. ZZ plants grow slowly and cannot use much.

How to Propagate ZZ Plant

ZZ plants propagate three ways, and all of them are slow. Division is the quickest: unpot a mature plant, separate the rhizome clusters by hand or with a clean knife making sure each piece has at least one stem and some roots, and pot each into dry cactus mix. Wait a week before the first watering so the cut surfaces callus.

Stem cuttings also work — take a whole stem at soil level, cut it into sections, and stand them in water or damp mix. Leaf cuttings are the slowest but most reproducible method: pull individual leaflets off with a small piece of the stem attached, lay them on barely moist mix, and wait. A pea-sized rhizome forms at the base after two to three months, and the first new shoot may take six months to a year. This is a propagation project for the patient; there is no way to speed it up, and warmth and bright indirect light are the only levers that help.

Common ZZ Plant Problems & Fixes

Yellowing leaves and stems going soft at the base

Usually means: Overwatering and rhizome rot. This is the classic ZZ failure and it is usually well advanced before anything shows above the soil.

What to do: Unpot immediately. Healthy rhizomes are firm and pale; rotted ones are brown, soft and smell sour. Cut away everything soft, let the remaining pieces dry for a day, and repot into dry cactus mix. Do not water for at least a week.

Wrinkled stems, leaflets curling or drooping

Usually means: Genuine underwatering — the plant has used up its stored reserves. This takes months to reach and is rare.

What to do: Water thoroughly until it drains, and repeat in a week. The stems plump back out over a couple of weeks. This is far easier to fix than the opposite problem.

Stems flopping outward from the centre

Usually means: Too little light causing weak, stretched growth, or a plant that has grown top-heavy and needs repotting.

What to do: Move to brighter indirect light. A snug, heavy pot helps keep tall stems upright. Stems that have flopped will not straighten, so prune them at soil level if they bother you.

Brown tips on the leaflets

Usually means: Fluoride and salts in tap water, over-fertilising, or occasionally very dry air combined with irregular watering.

What to do: Flush the pot thoroughly with plain water every few months, reduce fertiliser, and try filtered water if your supply is hard.

Leaflets dropping from otherwise healthy stems

Usually means: Sudden change in conditions — a move, a cold draught, or a dramatic shift in watering. ZZ plants shed leaflets as a stress response.

What to do: Settle it in one position, keep it away from doors and heating vents, and resume consistent care. New leaflets will not regrow on bare sections of stem, so prune those out once the plant has stabilised.

Pale, washed-out or scorched patches on leaflets

Usually means: Direct sun, particularly through an unshaded south or west window in summer.

What to do: Move it back from the glass or filter the light with a sheer curtain. Damaged leaflets will not recover; remove the worst and let new growth replace them.

Small brown bumps along stems and leaflet undersides

Usually means: Scale insects. The glossy foliage makes them relatively easy to spot once you look.

What to do: Scrape them off with a cotton bud dipped in rubbing alcohol and follow with neem oil, repeating weekly for a month to catch the crawlers.

Repotting ZZ Plant

ZZ plants are slow and happy being snug, so repotting every two to three years is plenty. The usual prompt is unmistakable: the rhizomes swell until they visibly distort or crack a plastic pot, or push the whole plant upward out of the soil.

Repot in spring into a pot one size larger, choosing something sturdy — a mature ZZ is heavy and top-heavy. Handle the rhizomes carefully; they bruise, and a damaged rhizome is an entry point for rot.

Use dry mix and wait a week before watering, which gives any roots damaged during the move a chance to seal. Skip fertiliser for a couple of months. Division into new pots is easiest at this point if the clump has become crowded.

ZZ Plant Varieties to Look For

The standard Zamioculcas zamiifolia has glossy mid-green leaflets. 'Raven' is the striking dark cultivar: new growth emerges bright lime green and matures to a near-black purple, and it needs decent indirect light to develop that colour fully.

'Zenzi' and 'Zamicro' are dwarf forms with shorter stems and tightly packed leaflets, useful for shelves and desks. A variegated form exists with cream and green marbling but is slow, expensive and more prone to scorching. All ZZ plants are toxic to cats and dogs, and the sap can irritate skin — worth wearing gloves when dividing rhizomes.
