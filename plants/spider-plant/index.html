About the Spider Plant

Classic houseplant with arching leaves and baby plantlets. Safe for pets and great for beginners.

Origin: South Africa

Care Tips

Bright indirect light, even moisture, produces babies.

Spider Plant Care in Depth

Light

Spider plant does best in bright indirect light, which keeps the variegation crisp and encourages the arching flower stems that carry the plantlets. It tolerates medium light and even fairly dim corners, but growth slows and the white stripes fade toward green.

Keep it out of direct midday sun, which bleaches and scorches the narrow leaves. An east-facing window is close to perfect; a west or south window works if the plant sits back from the glass or behind a sheer curtain. If you want babies, err on the brighter side — plantlets are produced far more freely in good light.

Watering through the year

Water when the top inch of mix has dried, keeping the soil lightly moist but never soggy. Through spring and summer that is typically once a week. Spider plants store water in thick, tuberous roots, so they cope with an occasional missed watering better than most.

Reduce to every ten to fourteen days in winter when growth slows. Water thoroughly until it drains and discard what collects in the saucer.

Spider plants are notoriously sensitive to fluoride and chlorine in tap water — this is the single most common cause of the brown tips they are famous for. If your water is heavily treated, use filtered, distilled or rainwater, or leave tap water standing uncovered overnight. Average household humidity is fine, though they appreciate a little extra.

Soil and potting mix

A standard well-draining potting mix with a handful of perlite suits them well. The thick storage roots want air as well as moisture, so avoid dense, compacted media.

Feed every four weeks through spring and summer with a balanced liquid fertiliser at half strength, and stop over winter. Go easy: over-fertilising is the second most common cause of brown leaf tips after tap water, because salts accumulate in the mix. Flushing the pot with plain water every few months clears them out.

How to Propagate Spider Plant

Spider plants are the easiest houseplant of all to propagate, because they do most of the work themselves. A mature plant sends out long arching stems carrying plantlets — miniature spider plants, often with stubby roots already forming at the base.

You have three options. Leave the plantlet attached to the parent and pin it down into a small pot of moist mix beside it; once it has rooted in a few weeks, snip the connecting stem. Or cut the plantlet off and stand it in water until roots are an inch or two long, then pot it up. Or plant the cut plantlet straight into damp soil and keep it moist while it establishes. All three work reliably; the attached method is the most foolproof because the parent keeps feeding the baby while it roots. Mature clumps can also be divided at repotting, which is the quickest way to make two full-sized plants.

Common Spider Plant Problems & Fixes

Brown tips on the leaves

Usually means: The signature spider plant complaint. Usually fluoride or chlorine in tap water, salt buildup from fertiliser, or dry air — often all three.

What to do: Switch to filtered, distilled or rainwater, flush the pot with plain water every few months, and cut back on feeding. Trim the brown tip at an angle following the leaf's natural point; it will not regrow green but it will stop spreading.

No plantlets being produced

Usually means: Insufficient light, a plant too young to flower, or too much root room. Spider plants tend to produce babies once they are comfortably pot-bound.

What to do: Move to brighter indirect light and resist repotting into a much larger container. A plant that is slightly snug flowers and produces plantlets far more readily.

Pale, washed-out leaves losing their stripes

Usually means: Either too little light, which fades variegation toward green, or too much direct sun, which bleaches the whole leaf.

What to do: Check the position. Faded-to-green usually means move brighter; bleached and papery means move back from the glass.

Leaves yellowing and limp, mix constantly damp

Usually means: Overwatering and the beginnings of root rot in the fleshy storage roots.

What to do: Let the top inch dry fully between waterings and confirm the pot drains freely. If the roots are brown and soft rather than firm and white, trim them and repot into fresh mix.

Whole plant looks limp and the pot feels very light

Usually means: Underwatering, or a root system so congested that there is barely any soil left to hold water.

What to do: Water thoroughly and check whether roots have filled the pot. A plant that dries out within two or three days of watering needs repotting regardless of the calendar.

Fine webbing between leaves, stippled grey-green foliage

Usually means: Spider mites, which favour the dry air around radiators.

What to do: Rinse the plant thoroughly in the shower, then treat with insecticidal soap every five to seven days for three cycles. Raising humidity makes recurrence much less likely.

Brown mushy patches at the base of the leaf clump

Usually means: Crown rot from water sitting in the centre of the rosette, or from soil kept saturated.

What to do: Water into the mix rather than over the crown, improve drainage, and remove affected leaves at the base. Divide out any healthy sections if the centre is badly affected.

Repotting Spider Plant

Spider plants grow vigorously and typically need repotting every one to two years. The thick white storage roots fill a pot quickly and will eventually lift the whole plant above the rim or crack a plastic container outright.

Repot in spring, moving up one pot size. Do not be tempted by a much larger container — a slightly snug spider plant produces far more plantlets, and extra soil just stays wet. Loosen the dense root mass, trim any brown or mushy roots, and set the plant at its previous depth.

This is the natural moment to divide. A congested clump pulls apart into two or three plants, each with roots and a good fan of leaves. Water well afterwards and wait a month before feeding.

Spider Plant Varieties to Look For

'Vittatum' is the classic, with a broad white central stripe and green margins. 'Variegatum' reverses that, with green centres and white edges. Both are equally easy and equally prolific with plantlets.

'Bonnie' is the curly form, with leaves that spiral tightly — the same plant behaviourally, just more compact and better suited to a shelf. 'Hawaiian' has a pale champagne-coloured flush on new growth that fades to green as leaves mature. All spider plants are non-toxic to cats and dogs, which is why they are one of the most recommended plants for pet households — though cats are notoriously drawn to chewing the dangling leaves, so expect some nibbling.
