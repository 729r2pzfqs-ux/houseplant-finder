About the Anthurium

Glossy heart-shaped flowers in red, pink, or white. Blooms year-round.

Origin: Central America

Care Tips

Bright indirect light, keep moist, high humidity.

Anthurium Care in Depth

Light

Anthurium wants bright indirect light. This is the main determinant of whether it produces the red, pink or white spathes it is grown for. In medium or low light it survives and produces leaves but flowers rarely or not at all.

Keep it out of direct sun, which scorches the leaves and bleaches the spathes. An east window is ideal; a south or west window works if the plant sits back or behind a sheer curtain. A well-lit anthurium in good conditions can flower more or less continuously, with each spathe lasting six to eight weeks.

Watering through the year

Water when the top inch of mix has dried, then water thoroughly and drain completely. In spring and summer that usually means every five to seven days; in winter, every ten to fourteen.

Anthurium is an epiphyte in the wild, growing in leaf litter and on tree trunks, so the roots want moisture and air together. They rot quickly in dense, saturated soil, and the first sign is usually yellowing lower leaves.

Humidity matters. Anthurium wants 60% or more and shows dry air as browning leaf edges and failure to flower. A humidifier makes a real difference; bathrooms often suit them well. They are also sensitive to salts and chlorine, so filtered or rainwater is worth using if leaf tips keep browning.

Soil and potting mix

Use a chunky, airy mix rather than standard compost — roughly equal parts orchid bark, perlite and peat or coir, much like an aroid mix. Some growers grow anthurium in almost pure bark with good results.

The pot must drain freely and should be snug; anthuriums flower better slightly pot-bound and rot in an oversized pot of wet mix. Feed every four to six weeks through spring and summer with a fertiliser higher in phosphorus to support flowering, at half strength. Stop in winter.

How to Propagate Anthurium

Anthurium is propagated by division, and this is the reliable home method. A mature plant develops multiple crowns from a shared base, each with its own roots and leaves.

Divide in spring while repotting. Slide the plant out, brush away the mix, and identify where separate crowns join. Pull or cut them apart, giving each division at least two or three leaves and a healthy share of roots. Pot each into fresh chunky mix, water lightly, and keep warm and humid for the first month while they establish — a clear bag loosely over the pot helps. Expect a pause before new growth.

Stem cuttings are also possible on plants that have developed a visible trunk with aerial roots. Cut a section with at least one node and an aerial root, and pot it into damp sphagnum moss until roots develop. Seed propagation is slow and rarely worthwhile at home, and hybrids do not come true from seed.

Common Anthurium Problems & Fixes

No flowers, only leaves

Usually means: Insufficient light most commonly, then lack of phosphorus, too large a pot, or low humidity.

What to do: Move to bright indirect light, feed with a higher-phosphorus fertiliser through the growing season, keep it slightly pot-bound, and raise humidity above 60%.

Brown, crispy leaf edges

Usually means: Low humidity, salts or chlorine in tap water, or over-fertilising.

What to do: Raise humidity, switch to filtered or rainwater, and flush the pot with clean water every couple of months. Reduce feeding if you have been generous.

Yellowing lower leaves

Usually means: Overwatering if the mix is wet and several leaves go at once; normal ageing if it is the oldest leaf alone.

What to do: Confirm the mix drains and dries slightly between waterings. Repot into a chunkier medium if it stays soggy.

Flowers small, green-tinged or short-lived

Usually means: Not enough light or nutrients. Spathes ageing to green before dying back is normal.

What to do: Increase light and feed through the growing season. Cut spent flower stalks back to the base to redirect energy.

Brown or black spots spreading on leaves

Usually means: Bacterial blight or fungal leaf spot, often following water left on the foliage in still air.

What to do: Remove affected leaves immediately with sterile scissors, improve air circulation, and water the mix rather than the plant. Isolate it from other plants if spots spread fast.

Leaves drooping with wet soil

Usually means: Root rot. Damaged roots cannot take up water so the plant wilts despite moisture.

What to do: Unpot, trim every soft brown root, and repot into fresh airy mix. Water sparingly until growth resumes.

Sticky residue and cottony white spots

Usually means: Mealybugs or scale, both of which favour the leaf undersides and stem bases.

What to do: Wipe with rubbing alcohol, treat with insecticidal soap weekly for a month, and check the undersides where they hide.

Repotting Anthurium

Repot every two years in spring, or when roots fill the pot and the plant lifts itself upward as the stem elongates. Anthuriums develop a visible trunk over time with aerial roots along it, which is normal.

Move up one size only into chunky, airy mix. When repotting, set the plant slightly deeper to cover the exposed lower stem and aerial roots, which encourages new root growth and keeps the plant stable.

Water lightly, keep humidity high for a few weeks, and wait six weeks before feeding. This is the natural moment to divide a plant that has formed several crowns.

Anthurium Varieties to Look For

Anthurium andraeanum is the flamingo flower, with glossy heart-shaped leaves and the familiar waxy red, pink, white or coral spathes. Anthurium scherzerianum, the pigtail anthurium, has a curled spadix and narrower leaves.

Foliage anthuriums are a different proposition entirely: Anthurium clarinervium has velvety dark leaves with striking white veining, and A. crystallinum is similar — both are grown for leaves rather than flowers and want higher humidity still. All anthuriums are toxic to cats and dogs, with calcium oxalate crystals causing mouth and throat irritation, so keep them out of reach in a pet household.
