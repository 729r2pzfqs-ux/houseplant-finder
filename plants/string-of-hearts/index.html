About the String of Hearts

Delicate trailing vines with tiny heart-shaped leaves. Can grow very long.

Origin: South Africa

Care Tips

Bright indirect light, water when dry, don't overwater.

String of Hearts Care in Depth

Light

String of hearts wants bright indirect light with a few hours of gentle direct sun. Light is what determines whether the strands stay dense with closely spaced leaves and strong silver marbling, or stretch into long bare threads.

An east window is close to ideal, or a south or west window with the plant set back slightly. In low light the internodes lengthen dramatically, leaves shrink, and the purple undersides fade. In too much harsh sun the leaves bleach and crisp. A well-lit plant develops noticeably pinker stems and deeper silver patterning.

Watering through the year

The thick leaves and the tubers along the stems store water, so treat this as a semi-succulent. Let the mix dry out almost completely before watering, then water thoroughly and drain. In spring and summer that is usually every two weeks; in winter, every three to four weeks.

Overwatering is the main cause of failure and shows as yellowing leaves and stems rotting at soil level. The leaves are a reliable gauge — firm and plump means well watered, slightly soft and dimpled means it is time.

Water at the soil rather than over the strands, and avoid letting water collect at the crown. Humidity is not important; average household air suits it well. This is a considerably more drought-tolerant plant than its delicate appearance suggests.

Soil and potting mix

Use a gritty, fast-draining mix — cactus compost with added perlite, or two parts succulent mix to one part coarse sand. The fine roots rot quickly in anything that holds water.

Keep the pot small and shallow; the root system is modest relative to the length of the strands, and excess soil stays wet. Hanging pots suit the plant well but check they drain and are not collecting water in a hidden reservoir. Feed at half strength once a month through spring and summer only.

How to Propagate String of Hearts

String of hearts is one of the most satisfying plants to propagate because it offers three distinct methods, including one unique to this species.

The tuber method is the most reliable. Look along the strands for small round grey-brown tubers, sometimes called aerial tubers or bulbils, growing at the nodes. Press one onto the surface of damp mix while it is still attached to the parent strand, and it will root within a few weeks, after which you can sever the connection. A tuber that has already fallen off can be half-buried the same way.

The butterfly method uses leaf pairs: cut the strand into segments each containing one pair of leaves and the node between them, lay them on damp mix with the node in contact, and each roots into a new plant. Ordinary strand cuttings also work — coil a length of strand on top of damp mix so several nodes touch the surface, which produces a full pot faster than a single cutting. Water propagation works but the transition to soil is often poor, so soil or moss is the better route.

Common String of Hearts Problems & Fixes

Long bare strands with widely spaced leaves

Usually means: Insufficient light. The strands stretch searching for it.

What to do: Move to a brighter position. The stretched sections will not fill in, so trim them and coil the cuttings back onto the soil surface to root and thicken the pot.

Leaves losing their silver marbling, turning plain green

Usually means: Too little light. The patterning and purple undersides are both light-dependent.

What to do: Increase light gradually. New growth comes in properly marked; existing leaves stay as they are.

Yellowing leaves and strands rotting at soil level

Usually means: Overwatering. The fine roots and crown rot readily in wet mix.

What to do: Stop watering, let the mix dry completely, and take healthy strand cuttings as insurance. Repot into a grittier mix in a smaller pot if the medium holds water too long.

Leaves thin, dimpled and soft

Usually means: Underwatering, which this plant tolerates well but not indefinitely.

What to do: Water thoroughly until it drains. Leaves plump up within a few days.

Plant bare and sparse at the top of the pot

Usually means: Natural ageing at the crown, combined with all the growth energy going into the long trailing strands.

What to do: Coil some of the existing strands back onto the soil surface so they root at the nodes, or add rooted cuttings. This is routine maintenance for a mature plant rather than a fault.

White cottony clusters among the strands

Usually means: Mealybugs, which hide extremely well in the tangle of fine stems and leaf pairs.

What to do: Dab each cluster with rubbing alcohol on a cotton bud. Inspect very thoroughly and weekly for a month — the dense strands make this one of the harder plants to clear.

Small flies around the pot

Usually means: Fungus gnats in soil kept too damp for this semi-succulent.

What to do: Let the mix dry out fully, add a grit top-dressing, and extend the watering interval substantially.

Repotting String of Hearts

String of hearts has a small root system and is happy snug, so repot only every two to three years, or when the tubers have filled the pot.

Choose a small, shallow pot with good drainage. Spring is the right time. The strands tangle and snap easily, so gather them loosely and lift them aside before working, and treat any that break off as free cuttings.

Use dry gritty mix, wait a week before the first watering, and skip fertiliser for a couple of months. Repotting is a good opportunity to coil some strands onto the fresh surface so they root and thicken a plant that has gone bare at the crown.

String of Hearts Varieties to Look For

Ceropegia woodii is the standard, with small heart-shaped leaves marbled silver over dark green and purple undersides. 'Variegata' adds cream and pink to the leaves and is slower, more delicate and noticeably more light-hungry.

Ceropegia linearis is a related species with narrower leaves. The plant is sometimes sold as rosary vine or chain of hearts. Ceropegia woodii is non-toxic to cats and dogs, which is a genuine advantage over most trailing plants — though the dangling strands are irresistible to cats and a high hanging position protects the plant more than the pet.
