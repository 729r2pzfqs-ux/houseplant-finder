About the Moon Cactus

Colorful grafted cactus in red, yellow, or orange. Limited lifespan.

Origin: South America

Care Tips

Bright indirect light, water sparingly, grafted plant has shorter life.

Moon Cactus Care in Depth

Light

Moon cactus needs bright indirect light — and this is one of the few cacti where full direct sun is actively harmful. The colourful top has no chlorophyll at all and cannot protect itself from intense light, so it scorches where a green cactus would be fine.

An east window is ideal, or a south window with a sheer curtain. In insufficient light the green rootstock below stretches and the graft becomes unstable. The essential thing to understand is that a moon cactus is two plants grafted together: a coloured, chlorophyll-free Gymnocalycium top sitting on a green Hylocereus rootstock that does all the photosynthesis and keeps it alive.

Watering through the year

Water thoroughly, then let the mix dry out completely before watering again — typically every two to three weeks in spring and summer, and once a month or less in winter.

Water at soil level and never let water sit against the graft union where the two plants meet. That join is the weak point of the whole plant, and moisture there causes rot that kills both halves.

The rootstock is the part that matters for watering, since it has the roots. A shrivelled green base means underwatering; a soft or discoloured base means overwatering. Humidity should be low. Moon cacti are often sold in tiny pots of peat that hold water badly, which is why so many fail within months of purchase.

Soil and potting mix

Use a gritty cactus mix — one part cactus compost to one part coarse sand or perlite. Repotting a newly bought moon cactus out of its shop peat and into proper gritty mix substantially improves its chances.

Use a small terracotta pot with drainage. Feed very sparingly, at most once or twice during the growing season with a half-strength cactus feed, and never in winter. The plant is small and slow and cannot use much.

How to Propagate Moon Cactus

Moon cactus cannot be propagated conventionally, because the coloured top has no chlorophyll and cannot survive on its own roots. Cutting it off and potting it simply results in a slow death as its stored energy runs out.

The only way to propagate the coloured portion is to graft it onto a new rootstock. If your plant's rootstock is failing but the coloured top is healthy, take a fresh Hylocereus or other columnar cactus cutting, slice the top flat, slice the base of the coloured scion flat, press the two cut surfaces together aligning the vascular rings as closely as you can, and hold them with a rubber band or light weight for a week or two in a dry, bright, warm spot.

Alignment of the inner vascular ring is what determines success; the join will fail if the rings do not overlap. The green rootstock itself can be propagated conventionally from cuttings. Offsets that appear on the coloured top can also be grafted, which is how they are produced commercially.

Common Moon Cactus Problems & Fixes

Coloured top shrivelling while the green base looks fine

Usually means: The graft is failing, or the top has simply exhausted itself — grafted moon cacti have a limited lifespan, often only a few years, because the two species grow at different rates.

What to do: There is no reliable fix once the graft fails. Regrafting onto fresh rootstock is possible but difficult. Treat a moon cactus as a temporary plant rather than a long-term one.

Soft brown tissue at the join between the two plants

Usually means: Rot at the graft union, usually from water sitting there.

What to do: Almost always fatal. Water at soil level only. If caught very early, cut above the rot and attempt a regraft.

Green rootstock stretching and thinning

Usually means: Insufficient light. The rootstock does all the photosynthesis and needs adequate light.

What to do: Move to bright indirect light. The stretched section is permanent and makes the plant unstable, so support may be needed.

Coloured top bleaching or developing pale scorched patches

Usually means: Direct sun. The chlorophyll-free tissue has no protection.

What to do: Move out of direct light into bright indirect conditions. Damage is permanent.

Green base soft and discoloured at soil level

Usually means: Overwatering, frequently made worse by the peat-based mix these are sold in.

What to do: Repot into gritty cactus mix in a terracotta pot immediately, cutting away any soft tissue. Water far less often.

Whole plant wobbling or the top falling off

Usually means: The graft union has failed mechanically.

What to do: The top cannot survive alone. Attempt a fresh graft if you want to keep it, or keep the rootstock, which grows on happily as an ordinary cactus.

Small offsets appearing on the coloured top

Usually means: Normal on a healthy graft.

What to do: These can be removed and grafted onto new rootstock, which is the only way to propagate the colour.

Repotting Moon Cactus

Repot a newly purchased moon cactus promptly, since they are almost always sold in a peat mix that holds far too much water. Beyond that, repot every two to three years.

Use a small terracotta pot with drainage and a gritty cactus mix. Handle the plant by the rootstock rather than the coloured top, and avoid putting any pressure on the graft union.

Use dry mix and wait a week before watering so damaged roots can seal. Plant at the same depth and keep the graft union well clear of the soil surface — burying it guarantees rot.

Moon Cactus Varieties to Look For

Moon cactus is Gymnocalycium mihanovichii friedrichii in its chlorophyll-free mutant forms — red, orange, yellow, pink or purple — grafted onto a green rootstock, usually Hylocereus undatus, the dragon fruit cactus.

The same technique produces grafted cacti in many colour combinations, and multi-headed grafts are sold as novelties. It is worth understanding what you are buying: this is a manufactured plant with a limited lifespan, typically one to three years, rather than a long-lived specimen. Moon cactus is non-toxic to cats and dogs, though the spines present the usual mechanical hazard.
