About the Nerve Plant

Delicate veined leaves in pink, white, or red. Faints when thirsty.

Origin: South America

Care Tips

Low light okay, keep moist, high humidity, terrariums.

Nerve Plant Care in Depth

Light

Nerve plant grows on the rainforest floor in Peru and wants medium indirect light with no direct sun. It is genuinely a low-to-medium light plant, which sets it apart from most brightly patterned foliage.

A north-facing window suits it, or a position well back from an east window. Direct sun scorches the thin leaves and bleaches the vein pattern. In light that is too low the leaves lose contrast and the plant becomes leggy, but this is a far less common problem than too much light. Fittonia also does well under artificial light, which makes it a good desk plant.

Watering through the year

This is the demanding part. Fittonia wants consistently moist soil and is famous for collapsing dramatically the moment it dries out — the entire plant goes limp and flat within hours, earning it the nickname drama queen.

Water when the surface feels barely dry, which in a warm room can be every two to three days. Use tepid water and avoid letting the plant wilt, because although it usually recovers within an hour of watering, repeated collapses damage the leaves permanently.

Humidity above 60% is genuinely needed rather than merely preferred. Dry air produces brown crispy edges and is the main reason nerve plants fail in ordinary rooms. A terrarium, a closed glass container or a bathroom solves this permanently, and Fittonia is one of the best terrarium plants available for exactly this reason.

Soil and potting mix

Use a light, moisture-retentive mix — peat or coir-based compost with added perlite. It needs to hold water consistently without compacting.

A shallow, wide pot suits the shallow spreading root system better than a deep one. Plastic or glazed ceramic conserves moisture better than terracotta, which dries far too fast for this species. Feed at quarter to half strength monthly through spring and summer only; fittonia is small and easily over-fertilised, which shows as browned leaf edges.

How to Propagate Nerve Plant

Nerve plant propagates very easily from stem cuttings, and it is worth doing regularly because plants go leggy and bare at the base within a year or two.

Take a cutting two to three inches long with several pairs of leaves, cutting just below a node, and strip the lowest pair. Root in water, where roots appear within one to two weeks, or push directly into damp mix.

Humidity is what determines success. Cover soil-rooted cuttings with a clear bag or propagator, or root them in a terrarium where conditions are already right. Pot several rooted cuttings together for a full plant — a single cutting takes a long time to look like anything. Division also works well on a mature clump: pull the plant apart into sections each with roots and several stems, and pot separately. Pinching the growing tips regularly encourages branching and delays the legginess.

Common Nerve Plant Problems & Fixes

Whole plant collapses flat, then recovers after watering

Usually means: The signature fittonia behaviour — the soil dried out. It is dramatic but usually not fatal.

What to do: Water immediately with tepid water and the plant typically revives within an hour. Water before this point rather than using the collapse as your cue; repeated wilting causes permanent leaf damage.

Brown, crispy leaf edges

Usually means: Low humidity, which is the fundamental problem with growing this plant in an ordinary room.

What to do: Raise humidity above 60% with a humidifier, or move the plant into a terrarium or closed container. A pebble tray alone is rarely enough for fittonia.

Leaves yellowing with wet soil

Usually means: Overwatering — consistently moist is not the same as waterlogged, and the fine roots rot easily.

What to do: Confirm the pot drains and is not standing in water. Aim for damp rather than soggy, and check the surface before each watering.

Leggy stems with bare stretches and few leaves

Usually means: Natural habit accelerated by low light, and a lack of pinching.

What to do: Pinch the growing tips regularly to force branching, and take cuttings from the healthy ends to replant into the same pot. Treat renewal as routine.

Faded vein pattern and washed-out leaves

Usually means: Too much light, usually direct sun.

What to do: Move to a shadier position. Faded leaves stay as they are, but new growth comes in properly marked.

Leaves dropping from the lower stems

Usually means: Repeated drying out, low humidity, or cold exposure. Fittonia dislikes temperatures below about 16°C.

What to do: Keep the soil consistently damp, raise humidity, and move away from draughty windows.

Small flies around the pot

Usually means: Fungus gnats breeding in permanently damp topsoil — an occupational hazard with a plant that must stay moist.

What to do: Use yellow sticky traps for the adults and a thin layer of horticultural grit or sand on the surface, which prevents egg-laying without letting the soil dry out.

Repotting Nerve Plant

Repot every one to two years in spring, or when roots fill the pot. Fittonia has a shallow spreading root system that fills sideways rather than downwards.

Choose a wide, shallow pot one size larger, in plastic or glazed ceramic to conserve moisture. Handle the fine roots gently.

Water with tepid water afterwards and keep humidity high for a few weeks. Do not feed for six weeks. This is the natural moment to divide a congested clump, and to take cuttings if the plant has gone bare in the middle — replanting cuttings around the parent thickens it quickly.

Nerve Plant Varieties to Look For

Fittonia albivenis comes in two main groups. The Verschaffeltii group has pink or red veining on deep green leaves; 'Red Anne' and 'Pink Star' are typical. The Argyroneura group has white or silver veining; 'White Anne' and the large-leaved 'Argyroneura' are common.

'Mini White' and other miniature forms are particularly well suited to terrariums and bottle gardens. Fittonia is non-toxic to cats and dogs, and combined with its small size and shade tolerance, it is a good choice for a desk or bathroom in a pet household — provided you can meet the humidity requirement.
