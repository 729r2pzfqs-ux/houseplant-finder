About the Peace Lily

Elegant white flowers and glossy leaves. One of the best air purifying plants. Droops dramatically when thirsty.

Origin: Central America

Care Tips

Low to medium light, keep moist, high humidity.

Peace Lily Care in Depth

Light

Peace lily is one of the few genuinely flowering plants that tolerates low light, which is why it ends up in office lobbies. It survives in dim corners, but it will not bloom there. The white spathes need medium to bright indirect light to form, so if yours has produced only leaves for a year, light is almost certainly the reason.

Keep it out of direct sun, which scorches the thin leaves within days. A north or east window, or a few feet back from a brighter one, gives the balance of enough light to flower without burning.

Watering through the year

Peace lily likes evenly moist soil and, unusually among houseplants, tells you clearly when it is thirsty: the whole plant droops dramatically, then recovers within hours of watering. Tempting as it is to use that as a schedule, repeated wilting stresses the plant and causes leaf-edge browning over time. Water when the top inch is dry rather than waiting for the collapse.

In spring and summer that is usually once a week; in winter, every ten to fourteen days. Water thoroughly until it drains and never leave the pot standing in a full saucer.

This is a plant that genuinely benefits from humidity. It comes from tropical forest floors and appreciates 50% or more — a pebble tray, a humidifier or a bathroom position all help. It is also sensitive to chlorine and fluoride, so if you see brown tips despite good watering, switch to filtered or rainwater.

Soil and potting mix

Use a standard peat-based potting mix with a handful of perlite for drainage. Peace lily wants a medium that holds moisture without becoming waterlogged, so avoid heavy garden soil and avoid pure bark mixes, which dry out far too fast for this species.

Feed every six to eight weeks during spring and summer with a balanced liquid fertiliser at half strength. Peace lilies are light feeders and over-fertilising is a common cause of brown leaf tips. Stop feeding completely in autumn and winter.

How to Propagate Peace Lily

Peace lily is propagated by division, not by cuttings. A leaf or stem cutting will not root, because the plant grows as a clump of crowns rising from a shared root mass rather than from nodes along a stem.

The best time is spring, ideally while repotting. Slide the plant out and look at the base: you will see distinct crowns, each with its own leaves and roots. Work them apart with your fingers where you can, and use a clean knife where the root mass is too dense. Each division needs at least two or three leaves and a decent share of roots to establish. Pot each one into fresh mix in a pot only slightly larger than its roots, water well, and keep out of direct light for a few weeks while it settles. Expect a sulk — some drooping and perhaps a lost leaf — before new growth appears.

Common Peace Lily Problems & Fixes

Plant wilts dramatically, then recovers after watering

Usually means: Underwatering. Peace lily collapses visibly when the mix dries out and bounces back quickly once watered.

What to do: Water more regularly, aiming to water when the top inch is dry rather than waiting for the wilt. Repeated wilting damages leaf tissue even though the plant recovers each time.

Brown, crispy leaf tips

Usually means: Chlorine or fluoride in tap water, over-fertilising, or dry air — often a combination.

What to do: Switch to filtered, distilled or rainwater, halve your fertiliser, flush the pot with plain water every few months, and raise humidity. Trim brown tips at an angle with clean scissors.

No flowers for a year or more

Usually means: Not enough light. Peace lily survives in low light but only blooms in medium to bright indirect light. A very young plant may also simply be too immature.

What to do: Move to a brighter spot out of direct sun and feed lightly through the growing season. Blooming usually follows within a few months.

Flowers emerging green instead of white, or turning green as they age

Usually means: Green-ageing is normal — spathes turn green then brown as they finish. Flowers that open green usually mean too much fertiliser or too much light.

What to do: Cut spent flower stalks to the base. If new blooms open green, reduce feeding and check the plant is not in harsh light.

Yellowing leaves, especially lower ones

Usually means: Overwatering if the mix stays wet, natural ageing if it is one or two older leaves at a time, or too much direct light if the yellowing is widespread and pale.

What to do: Check the soil first. If it is soggy, extend the watering interval and confirm the pot drains. Remove yellow leaves at the base.

Black or dark brown leaf bases, mushy stems

Usually means: Root rot from waterlogged soil or a pot left standing in water.

What to do: Unpot, cut away every soft brown root, and repot into fresh mix in a pot with working drainage. Water sparingly until new growth appears.

Sticky residue on leaves and fine webbing

Usually means: Spider mites or mealybugs, both encouraged by dry indoor air.

What to do: Wipe the leaves down, rinse the plant in the shower, and treat with insecticidal soap every five to seven days for three cycles. Raising humidity discourages spider mites specifically.

Repotting Peace Lily

Repot every one to two years, in spring. Peace lily grows into its pot quickly and the signs are clear: roots circling the surface or emerging from drainage holes, water running straight through, and much faster wilting between waterings than before.

Go up only one pot size. Peace lily flowers more freely when slightly snug, and an oversized pot holds wet soil around the roots. Loosen the root ball, remove any dark soft roots, and set the plant at the same depth in fresh mix.

If the clump has become congested, repotting is the natural moment to divide it into two or three plants. Water thoroughly afterwards and hold off fertiliser for six weeks while the roots recover.

Peace Lily Varieties to Look For

'Mauna Loa' is the common large form, reaching around three feet with big spathes. 'Domino' carries irregular white variegation across the leaves and needs a little more light to keep the pattern. 'Sensation' is the giant of the group, capable of four to six feet with broad, deeply ribbed leaves — a genuine floor plant.

For smaller spaces, 'Petite' stays under a foot. Whichever you choose, all Spathiphyllum are toxic to cats and dogs: the calcium oxalate crystals cause mouth and throat irritation if chewed, so this is not a plant for a household with a nibbling pet.
