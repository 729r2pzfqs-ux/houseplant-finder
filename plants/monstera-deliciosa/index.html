About the Monstera Deliciosa

The Monstera Deliciosa is famous for its stunning split leaves and easy care. A statement plant that can grow quite large.

Origin: Central America

Care Tips

Bright indirect light, water when top inch dry, mist occasionally.

Monstera Deliciosa Care in Depth

Light

Monstera deliciosa grows under the canopy in Central American rainforests, climbing tree trunks toward gaps in the leaves above. That habit explains what it wants indoors: bright, filtered light rather than bare sun. An east window, or a spot two to three feet back from a south or west window, is close to ideal.

Light is also what controls fenestration — the famous holes and splits. Juvenile leaves are solid hearts, and the plant only starts producing split leaves once it has enough light and maturity to support them. A Monstera in a dim corner will stay alive for years and never fenestrate. If yours is pushing out small, solid leaves, low light is the first thing to correct.

Watering through the year

Water when the top two inches of mix have dried out. In a bright spot through spring and summer that usually lands somewhere around every seven to ten days, but treat that as a starting point rather than a schedule — pot size, mix and light all move the number. Water thoroughly until it runs from the drainage holes, then empty the saucer.

From late autumn through winter, growth slows and the mix stays wet far longer. Stretch the interval to every two or three weeks and check with a finger each time rather than watering on autopilot. More Monsteras are killed by winter watering habits carried over from summer than by any pest.

Monstera tolerates average household humidity, but it grows faster and produces better leaves above 60%. Grouping plants, a pebble tray or a humidifier all help. Misting looks appealing but raises humidity for only a few minutes.

Soil and potting mix

Use a chunky, fast-draining aroid mix rather than straight potting soil. A reliable blend is roughly one third quality potting mix, one third orchid bark and one third perlite, with a couple of handfuls of coco coir or worm castings for moisture retention and nutrition. The bark keeps air pockets around the roots, which is exactly what an epiphytic climber expects.

Whatever you mix, the pot must have drainage holes. Monstera roots rot quickly in a sealed cachepot that collects water at the bottom. Feed with a balanced liquid fertiliser at half strength every four weeks during spring and summer, and stop entirely over winter.

How to Propagate Monstera Deliciosa

Monstera propagates readily from stem cuttings, and the single thing that determines success is whether your cutting includes a node. The node is the slightly raised band on the stem where a leaf meets it, often with a brown aerial root emerging. A leaf with no node will sit in water looking healthy for months and never root, because all the growth tissue is in the node.

Cut just below a node with clean shears, taking one or two leaves with it. Put the cutting in water, keeping the node submerged and the leaves out, in bright indirect light. Change the water weekly. Roots usually appear within two to four weeks; once several are two to three inches long, pot into your usual aroid mix and keep it slightly moister than normal for the first fortnight while the plant transitions from water roots to soil roots. You can also root cuttings directly in damp sphagnum moss, which skips that transition entirely and tends to produce sturdier plants.

Common Monstera Deliciosa Problems & Fixes

New leaves have no splits or holes

Usually means: Not enough light, or a plant that is simply still young. Fenestration develops with maturity and adequate light, and it will not appear on a plant kept in a dim corner.

What to do: Move it closer to a bright window with filtered light and give it something to climb — a moss pole encourages the plant to produce larger, more mature leaves. Expect the change to show on new growth, not on existing leaves.

Yellowing lower leaves

Usually means: Most often overwatering, especially if several leaves yellow at once and the mix is still damp days after watering. A single old leaf yellowing near the base is just normal ageing.

What to do: Check the mix before every watering and let the top two inches dry out. If the soil smells sour or the roots are brown and soft, repot into fresh, chunky mix and trim the damaged roots.

Brown, crispy leaf edges

Usually means: Low humidity, underwatering, or salt buildup from fertiliser and hard tap water.

What to do: Raise humidity, water more consistently, and flush the pot thoroughly with plain water every few months to wash out accumulated salts.

Dark brown patches with yellow halos

Usually means: Bacterial or fungal leaf spot, usually following water sitting on the foliage in a cool, still room.

What to do: Remove affected leaves, improve air circulation, and water the soil rather than the leaves. Avoid misting until it clears.

Fine webbing and stippled, dull leaves

Usually means: Spider mites, which thrive in dry indoor air and are easy to miss until the webbing appears.

What to do: Rinse the plant in the shower, then treat every five to seven days with insecticidal soap or neem oil for at least three cycles to catch newly hatched mites. Raising humidity makes reinfestation less likely.

White cottony clusters in leaf joints

Usually means: Mealybugs, which shelter where the leaf stem meets the main stem and in the folds of new growth.

What to do: Dab each cluster with a cotton bud dipped in rubbing alcohol, then follow with insecticidal soap. Check weekly for a month — they hide well and one missed cluster restarts the infestation.

Stems growing long and leggy with small leaves

Usually means: Insufficient light, and often a lack of support. Monstera is a climber, and an unsupported plant sprawls rather than building size.

What to do: Increase light and add a moss pole or trellis, tying the stem in so aerial roots can grip. Prune leggy sections back to a node and propagate the cuttings.

Repotting Monstera Deliciosa

Repot every two to three years, or when roots grow out of the drainage holes, water runs straight through without wetting the mix, or the plant dries out far faster than it used to. Spring and early summer are the best time, so the plant recovers during active growth.

Go up only one pot size — roughly two inches wider in diameter. An oversized pot holds more wet mix than the roots can use, which is a common route to root rot. Slide the plant out, loosen the outer roots, cut away anything brown and mushy, and settle it into fresh aroid mix at the same depth it was growing before.

This is also the natural moment to add or replace a moss pole, since you can position it against the stem while the root ball is out of the pot. Water thoroughly after repotting, then hold off on fertiliser for four to six weeks while the roots settle.

Monstera Deliciosa Varieties to Look For

The plain species is the most common, but several forms are worth knowing. 'Thai Constellation' carries creamy, speckled variegation, is tissue-cultured, and is more stable and more widely available than it once was. The 'Albo Variegata' form produces dramatic blocks of pure white, propagates only by cutting, and is far more temperamental — a cutting with too much white lacks the chlorophyll to sustain itself.

Do not confuse Monstera deliciosa with Monstera adansonii, sold as the Swiss cheese vine, which has smaller leaves with enclosed holes rather than splits that reach the edge. Young deliciosa plants are also frequently mislabelled as split-leaf philodendron, a different genus entirely. 'Borsigiana' is a faster, more compact growth form of deliciosa rather than a separate species.
