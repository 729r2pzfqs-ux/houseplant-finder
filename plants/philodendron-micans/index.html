About the Philodendron Micans

A stunning trailing philodendron with velvety, iridescent leaves that shift between bronze, green, and purple tones.

Origin: Central America, Caribbean

Care Tips

Medium to bright indirect light, keep soil lightly moist, loves humidity.

Philodendron Micans Care in Depth

Light

Philodendron micans wants bright indirect light. The velvety leaves have a soft iridescent sheen and a bronze-to-deep-green colour that develops best in good light — in shade the leaves come out plain green and lose the coppery tones.

An east window is ideal, or a few feet back from a south or west window. Direct sun is particularly damaging to this species: the velvet texture comes from fine hairs on the leaf surface that scorch easily, leaving dull bleached patches. Filtered brightness is the target. In low light the vine stretches and the leaves shrink.

Watering through the year

Water when the top inch of mix has dried, then water thoroughly and drain. In spring and summer that is usually every seven to ten days; in winter, every two weeks.

Micans has thinner leaves than the standard heartleaf philodendron and shows thirst faster, with the leaves going limp and curling slightly. It recovers within hours of watering, but repeated drying causes permanent crisping on the delicate velvet surface.

Humidity above 50% produces noticeably better leaf quality and larger leaves — more so than for plain heartleaf philodendron, because the velvet texture suffers in dry air. Do not mist, though: water droplets sitting on the fine hairs cause spotting. Use a pebble tray or humidifier instead.

Soil and potting mix

Use a well-draining aroid mix — potting compost with added perlite and orchid bark. The roots want air alongside moisture.

Drainage holes are essential and the pot should be snug. Feed monthly at half strength through spring and summer, stopping in winter. Micans grows well trailing from a shelf or hanging pot, but a moss pole produces noticeably larger leaves, since like most philodendrons it is a climber that responds to support.

How to Propagate Philodendron Micans

Philodendron micans propagates as easily as plain heartleaf philodendron, and it is a good plant for a first attempt at velvet-leaved aroids.

Take a four to six inch cutting with two or three leaves, cutting half an inch below a node, and strip the lowest leaf so the node is bare. Root in water with the node submerged and leaves clear, changing the water weekly — roots appear within one to two weeks.

Soil or sphagnum propagation tends to produce sturdier roots for velvet-leaved philodendrons and avoids the brief adjustment period water-rooted cuttings go through. Keep humidity up while rooting. Pot three or four rooted cuttings together for an immediately full plant, and tuck extras back into the parent pot to thicken a vine that has gone bare at the crown. Avoid handling the leaves more than necessary, since the velvet surface marks.

Common Philodendron Micans Problems & Fixes

Leaves losing their velvety sheen and bronze tones

Usually means: Insufficient light. The iridescence and copper colouring are light responses.

What to do: Move to brighter indirect light. New growth comes in properly coloured; existing leaves stay as they are.

Dull bleached patches on the leaf surface

Usually means: Sun scorch. The fine hairs that create the velvet texture burn readily in direct light.

What to do: Move out of direct sun into filtered brightness. Damaged patches do not recover; remove badly affected leaves.

Water spots or marks on the leaves

Usually means: Misting, or water splashed onto the velvet surface, which does not shed water the way a glossy leaf does.

What to do: Stop misting and water at soil level. Raise humidity with a pebble tray or humidifier instead. Existing marks are permanent.

Brown crispy leaf edges

Usually means: Low humidity or inconsistent watering — micans is less forgiving than plain heartleaf philodendron.

What to do: Raise humidity above 50% and water on a more regular schedule.

Long bare vine with small leaves

Usually means: Too little light and no support to climb.

What to do: Increase light and add a moss pole. Prune the bare sections back to a node and root the cuttings back into the pot.

Yellowing leaves with damp soil

Usually means: Overwatering and the start of root rot.

What to do: Let the top inch dry before watering and confirm the pot drains. Repot into a chunkier mix if it stays soggy.

White cottony clusters along the stems

Usually means: Mealybugs, which hide in the leaf sheaths and around the nodes.

What to do: Dab each with rubbing alcohol on a cotton bud. Be gentle around the velvet leaves, which mark easily. Check weekly for a month.

Repotting Philodendron Micans

Repot every one to two years in spring. Micans grows steadily in good conditions, and the signs are roots through the drainage holes and rapid drying between waterings.

Move up one pot size only into fresh aroid mix. If adding a moss pole, do it now while the root ball is out.

Handle the velvet leaves as little as possible — they mark from handling as well as from water. Water thoroughly, keep humidity up, and wait a month before feeding. This is also the moment to cut back a leggy vine and replant rooted cuttings to restore density.

Philodendron Micans Varieties to Look For

Philodendron hederaceum var. hederaceum 'Micans' is the velvet-leaf philodendron — the same species as the common heartleaf philodendron, but a velvety variety with bronze-green iridescent leaves and pink-tinged new growth.

Related velvet-leaved philodendrons include P. gloriosum, a crawling species with white-veined heart-shaped leaves, P. verrucosum with hairy red petioles, and P. melanochrysum, the black gold philodendron, with long dark velvet leaves. All need higher humidity than glossy-leaved philodendrons. Every philodendron is toxic to cats and dogs, causing mouth irritation and drooling if chewed.
