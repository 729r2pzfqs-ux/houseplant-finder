About the Croton

Vibrant multicolored leaves in red, orange, yellow, and green.

Origin: Southeast Asia

Care Tips

Bright direct light, keep moist, high humidity, hates moving.

Croton Care in Depth

Light

Croton needs a lot of light — this is the entire basis of its reputation for difficulty. The vivid red, orange and yellow colouring only develops and persists in bright light, including several hours of direct sun. A south or west-facing window is usually required.

In insufficient light the plant does not die; it simply turns green. New leaves emerge without the colour that made you buy it, and the plant becomes leggy. If your croton is greening up, light is the answer and nothing else will fix it. Introduce strong sun gradually to a plant that has been in dimmer conditions, since abrupt changes cause leaf drop.

Watering through the year

Keep the mix evenly moist through spring and summer, watering when the top inch is dry — usually every five to seven days. Croton dislikes drying out completely and responds to drought by dropping leaves, often dramatically.

Reduce to every ten to fourteen days in winter, but still do not let the root ball go bone dry. The balance to strike is consistently moist without being soggy; both extremes cause the same symptom of leaf drop.

Humidity above 50% is important. Croton is a tropical shrub and dry indoor air, particularly in winter, is the main trigger for both leaf drop and spider mites. A humidifier makes a visible difference. Keep it away from draughts and cold windows — it resents temperatures below about 15°C.

Soil and potting mix

Use a rich, well-draining potting mix with added perlite — roughly three parts compost to one part perlite. The plant wants moisture retention with free drainage.

Feed every two to four weeks through spring and summer with a balanced fertiliser at half strength. Crotons are moderately hungry and good feeding supports the strong colouring. Stop completely in autumn and winter. Make sure the pot drains; croton roots rot in standing water.

How to Propagate Croton

Croton propagates from stem cuttings taken in spring or early summer, and warmth is the deciding factor in whether they root. Take a tip cutting four to six inches long with three or four leaves, cutting just below a node.

The cut bleeds a milky latex that irritates skin — wear gloves and rinse the cut end until it stops before potting. Remove the lowest leaves, dip the end in rooting hormone, and insert into damp, free-draining mix. Cover with a clear bag or propagator to hold humidity, and keep it somewhere genuinely warm; croton cuttings root poorly below about 21°C. Bright indirect light rather than direct sun while rooting. Expect four to eight weeks. Air layering is the more reliable option for a tall, leggy plant and produces a large plant immediately: wound the stem, pack damp sphagnum moss around it inside a plastic sleeve, and cut below the roots once they fill the moss.

Common Croton Problems & Fixes

Sudden dropping of many leaves

Usually means: The signature croton behaviour. Triggered by any abrupt change — being brought home from the shop, a move, a cold draught, or the soil drying out completely.

What to do: Settle it in one bright, warm, draught-free position and keep watering consistent. Some drop after purchase is normal and the plant usually recovers. Resist moving it again while it adjusts.

New leaves coming in plain green

Usually means: Not enough light. Colour production requires high light levels.

What to do: Move to the brightest window available, increasing exposure gradually. Existing green leaves will not colour up, but new growth will come in correctly.

Fine webbing, stippled dull leaves

Usually means: Spider mites, the most common croton pest, driven by dry air.

What to do: Rinse the whole plant thoroughly including leaf undersides, treat with insecticidal soap every five days for three cycles, and fix the humidity — otherwise they return immediately.

Brown, crispy leaf edges and tips

Usually means: Low humidity or underwatering, often combined with a position near a radiator.

What to do: Raise humidity, water more consistently, and move away from heat sources.

Leaves wilting despite damp soil

Usually means: Root rot from overwatering or poor drainage.

What to do: Check the roots, trim anything soft and brown, and repot into fresh free-draining mix. Water sparingly until new growth resumes.

Leggy stems with leaves only at the tips

Usually means: Insufficient light combined with no pruning.

What to do: Increase light and prune back by a third in spring. Croton branches readily from below the cut, and the removed sections can be rooted or air-layered.

Pale, washed-out or scorched leaf patches

Usually means: Sunburn after an abrupt move into strong direct sun.

What to do: Increase sun exposure gradually over two to three weeks rather than all at once. Remove badly scorched leaves.

Repotting Croton

Repot every one to two years in spring while the plant is growing, then less often. Signs are roots through the drainage holes and water running straight through the pot.

Move up one size only. Croton dislikes root disturbance and often drops leaves after repotting, so handle the root ball gently and keep disturbance minimal. Expect a sulk regardless.

Water thoroughly afterwards, keep humidity high and the plant out of direct sun for a couple of weeks while it recovers, then return it to full light. Do not fertilise for six weeks. If it starts shedding after repotting, hold your nerve and keep conditions stable rather than intervening further.

Croton Varieties to Look For

Codiaeum variegatum has dozens of named forms distinguished mainly by leaf shape. 'Petra' is the common one, with broad oval leaves veined in yellow, orange and red. 'Mammy' has narrow twisting leaves in deep reds and purples. 'Gold Dust' is green speckled with yellow and is among the more shade-tolerant.

'Zanzibar' has very narrow, almost grassy leaves in mixed colours. All crotons are toxic to cats and dogs, and the milky sap irritates human skin and can stain — gloves are worth wearing for pruning. The sap is also the reason this plant should be kept away from children who might break a leaf.
