About the Purple Shamrock

Purple triangular leaves that fold at night. Delicate pink flowers.

Origin: Brazil

Care Tips

Medium light, water when dry, may go dormant.

Purple Shamrock Care in Depth

Light

Purple shamrock wants bright indirect light with a few hours of gentle direct sun. Light is what keeps the deep purple colour saturated and the plant compact.

An east or south window works well, filtered in the hottest part of summer. In insufficient light the stems stretch into long thin stalks that flop over, and the purple pales. One of this plant's charms is its nyctinasty — the triangular leaflets fold down like closed umbrellas at night and open again in the morning, a daily rhythm driven by light that is quite visible and happens reliably in a well-lit plant.

Watering through the year

Water when the top inch of mix has dried, then water thoroughly and drain. In spring and summer that is usually every five to seven days. Oxalis grows from small bulb-like rhizomes and rots if kept saturated.

The important seasonal point is dormancy. Oxalis triangularis naturally goes dormant once or twice a year, usually after a period of heavy growth or in response to heat. The foliage dies back completely and the plant looks dead. This is normal and expected — do not discard it.

When dormancy begins, stop watering, cut the dead foliage back to soil level, and put the pot somewhere cool and dark for four to six weeks. Then bring it back into the light and resume watering, and fresh growth appears within a couple of weeks. Humidity is not important; average household air suits it.

Soil and potting mix

Use a standard well-draining potting mix with added perlite. The rhizomes want a medium that drains freely rather than staying wet.

Drainage holes are essential. Feed monthly at half strength through the active growing period and stop entirely during dormancy. Oxalis is not a heavy feeder. A pot that is wide rather than deep suits the shallow spreading rhizomes, and a crowded pot actually looks better since the foliage forms a dense mound.

How to Propagate Purple Shamrock

Oxalis triangularis is propagated by dividing the rhizomes, which is easy and very reliable — the plant multiplies readily on its own.

The best time is at the end of a dormant period, just before you bring the pot back into growth. Tip the pot out and sift through the mix: you will find small pale rhizomes that look like tiny pine cones or scaly bulbs, usually far more of them than you expected.

Separate them and pot them in groups of five or six, about an inch deep, in fresh well-draining mix. Water lightly and place in bright light. Shoots appear within one to three weeks. Because each rhizome produces a cluster of leaves, planting several together gives a full pot immediately rather than a sparse one. Handle them gently — they snap easily, though broken pieces often grow anyway. Individual rhizomes can also be shared or stored dry for a few weeks between plantings.

Common Purple Shamrock Problems & Fixes

All foliage dies back and the plant looks dead

Usually means: Dormancy, which is a normal part of this plant's cycle and happens once or twice a year, often triggered by heat.

What to do: Stop watering, cut the dead growth back to soil level, and rest the pot somewhere cool and dark for four to six weeks. Then resume light and water. Do not throw the plant away — the rhizomes are alive.

Long floppy stems that cannot hold the leaves up

Usually means: Insufficient light. The stalks stretch and weaken.

What to do: Move to a brighter position with some gentle direct sun. Cut the floppy growth back to soil level and the plant regrows compact from the rhizomes.

Purple colour fading toward green or bronze

Usually means: Too little light. The purple pigment is a light response.

What to do: Increase light gradually. New growth comes in properly coloured.

Leaves not opening during the day

Usually means: Too little light, or the plant entering dormancy.

What to do: Move somewhere brighter. If the leaves also begin yellowing and dying back, it is dormancy rather than a light problem, and should be allowed to run its course.

Mushy stems and rhizomes

Usually means: Overwatering, particularly if it continued into dormancy when the plant needs none.

What to do: Stop watering, let the mix dry out, and sift out any firm healthy rhizomes to replant in fresh mix. Discard soft ones.

Brown crispy leaf edges

Usually means: Underwatering, or scorch from harsh direct sun.

What to do: Water more consistently and filter the strongest midday light. Damaged leaves can be trimmed at the base; the plant replaces foliage rapidly.

Powdery white coating on the leaves

Usually means: Powdery mildew, encouraged by crowded growth and poor air circulation.

What to do: Remove affected leaves, thin the clump, and improve airflow. Avoid wetting the foliage when watering.

Repotting Purple Shamrock

Repot every one to two years, ideally at the end of a dormant period when the rhizomes can be handled without disturbing active growth.

Choose a wide, shallow pot rather than a deep one, since the rhizomes spread sideways near the surface. Move up one size, or simply refresh the mix in the same pot and replant a selection of the best rhizomes.

Plant the rhizomes about an inch deep and several to a pot for a full look. Water lightly until growth appears, then resume normal care. This is also the easiest moment to divide and share the surplus, of which there is usually plenty.

Purple Shamrock Varieties to Look For

Oxalis triangularis is the purple shamrock, with deep burgundy triangular leaflets and small pale pink or white flowers. 'Atropurpurea' is the darkest form. A green-leaved form, Oxalis regnellii, has the same habit with plain green foliage and white flowers.

Oxalis tetraphylla, the iron cross plant, has green leaves with a dark purple marking at the centre of each leaflet. The plant is sometimes sold as false shamrock or love plant. Important safety note: Oxalis contains oxalic acid and is toxic to cats and dogs in quantity, causing drooling, vomiting and in serious cases kidney effects — despite its delicate appearance and common gift-plant status it is worth keeping out of reach.
