About the Blue Star Fern

Blue-green wavy fronds. One of the easiest ferns to grow.

Origin: Americas

Care Tips

Low to medium light, keep moist but not soggy.

Blue Star Fern Care in Depth

Light

Blue star fern wants medium indirect light and, unusually for a fern, tolerates lower light well. It grows as an epiphyte on tree trunks in South American rainforests, so it is adapted to dappled shade.

A north or east window suits it well. Direct sun scorches the fronds and bleaches the distinctive blue-green colour. In deep shade it survives but produces fewer new fronds. This is one of the more forgiving ferns about light, and light problems are far less common with it than with maidenhair.

Watering through the year

Water when the top inch of mix has dried, then water thoroughly and drain fully. In spring and summer that is usually every seven to ten days; in winter, every two weeks or so.

Blue star fern is noticeably more drought-tolerant than most ferns thanks to its fleshy creeping rhizome, which stores some water. It is also considerably more prone to root rot than to drying out, so err on the dry side — this is the opposite of the advice for Boston and maidenhair ferns.

Humidity above 50% is preferred but not critical, which is the main reason this fern succeeds in ordinary rooms where others fail. It is sensitive to salts and chlorine in tap water, showing brown frond tips, so filtered or rainwater is worth using.

Soil and potting mix

As an epiphyte, blue star fern wants a loose, airy, bark-based medium rather than ordinary compost — a mix of orchid bark, perlite and some coir or sphagnum works well. Dense potting soil suffocates the rhizome and causes rot.

The pot should be shallow and wide, since the rhizome creeps across the surface rather than rooting deeply. Feed at quarter to half strength monthly through spring and summer only. Ferns are sensitive to fertiliser salts, and this one shows excess as browned frond edges.

How to Propagate Blue Star Fern

Blue star fern is propagated by dividing the rhizome, which is straightforward and reliable — considerably easier than most ferns.

The rhizome is the distinctive furry, silver-blue creeping stem that runs across the surface of the medium, with fronds emerging from it at intervals. It is one of the plant's most attractive features and should sit on top of the mix, not be buried.

In spring, slide the plant out and cut the rhizome into sections with a clean knife, making sure each piece has at least two or three fronds and some roots beneath it. Lay each section on top of fresh bark-based mix, pressing it lightly into contact but not burying it, and pin it down with a bent wire if it will not stay put. Water lightly, keep humidity up for a few weeks, and new fronds appear within a month or two. Spore propagation is possible but slow and unnecessary given how well division works.

Common Blue Star Fern Problems & Fixes

Brown frond tips

Usually means: Salts or chlorine in tap water, low humidity, or over-fertilising.

What to do: Switch to filtered or rainwater, flush the pot with clean water every couple of months, and reduce feeding to quarter strength. Trim tips following the frond shape.

Fronds yellowing with the medium staying wet

Usually means: Overwatering and rhizome rot — the main way this fern is killed, since people water it like a Boston fern.

What to do: Let the top inch dry properly between waterings. Check the rhizome: firm and furry is healthy, soft and brown is rot. Repot into a chunkier bark mix if the medium holds water.

Rhizome buried under the medium and rotting

Usually means: Planted too deep. The creeping rhizome must sit on the surface.

What to do: Repot with the rhizome exposed on top of the mix. This is the most common potting error with this species.

Fronds pale and bleached

Usually means: Too much light, usually direct sun.

What to do: Move to a shadier position. The blue-green colouring returns on new growth.

Brown dots in rows on frond undersides

Usually means: Spores, which are entirely normal on a mature fern and a sign of health.

What to do: No action needed. Irregularly scattered bumps that scrape off are scale instead.

New fronds emerging small or deformed

Usually means: Low humidity, over-fertilising, or physical damage to the delicate unfurling fronds.

What to do: Raise humidity slightly, reduce feeding, and avoid brushing past new growth.

Whole fronds browning and dying back

Usually means: Severe drought, or rhizome rot at the other extreme.

What to do: Check the rhizome and medium. Dry and shrunken means soak the pot; wet and soft means cut away rot and repot into an airier mix.

Repotting Blue Star Fern

Repot every two to three years in spring, or when the rhizome has crept across the whole surface and over the pot edge.

Choose a shallow, wide pot — depth is wasted on a surface-creeping rhizome and simply holds wet medium. Use fresh bark-based epiphytic mix.

The critical point: lay the rhizome on top of the mix rather than burying it, with only the roots below the surface. Water lightly, keep humidity up for a few weeks, and wait six weeks before feeding. This is the natural moment to divide a plant that has outgrown its pot.

Blue Star Fern Varieties to Look For

Phlebodium aureum is the blue star fern, with wavy-edged blue-green fronds on a furry silver rhizome. 'Mandaianum' has strongly ruffled frond margins and 'Davana' is a compact form.

The plant is also sold as golden polypody or hare's foot fern — though that last name more properly belongs to Davallia, a related epiphyte with similar furry rhizomes. Compared with other ferns, blue star is the most tolerant of dry air and irregular watering, making it the sensible choice for someone who has killed a maidenhair. All Phlebodium are non-toxic to cats and dogs.
