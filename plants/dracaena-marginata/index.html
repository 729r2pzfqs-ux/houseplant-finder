Spiky palm-like leaves on tall canes. Easy and architectural.

Origin: Madagascar

Care Tips

Medium indirect light, water when dry, tolerates neglect.

Dragon Tree Care in Depth

Light

Dracaena marginata tolerates medium light and grows best in bright indirect light. It is one of the more shade-tolerant tall houseplants, which is why it appears so often in offices, but growth in dim conditions is slow and the stems stretch toward the window.

Keep it out of direct sun, which bleaches the narrow leaves and produces dry brown patches. The red-edged varieties hold their colour better in bright light; in shade the margins dull toward green. Rotate the pot every few weeks, because this plant leans noticeably toward its light source and develops a permanent bend if left facing one way.

Watering through the year

Let the top two inches of mix dry out before watering. In spring and summer that usually means every ten to fourteen days, which is less often than most people expect from a plant this size. Dracaenas store water in their thick canes and are considerably more drought-tolerant than they look.

In winter, extend to every three to four weeks. Overwatering is the dominant cause of failure, showing first as yellowing lower leaves and soft stem bases.

Water quality is the other critical factor. Dracaenas are among the most fluoride-sensitive houseplants, and fluoridated tap water produces the dead brown leaf tips that almost every specimen eventually develops. Use filtered, distilled or rainwater and the problem largely disappears. Average humidity is fine, though above 40% is preferred.

Soil and potting mix

A standard well-draining potting mix with added perlite suits it. Avoid anything heavy or peat-dense that holds water around the roots, and avoid mixes containing superphosphate, which is a fluoride source.

Feed only every two months at half strength through spring and summer, and not at all in winter. Dracaenas are light feeders and sensitive to salt accumulation, which compounds the tip-burn problem. Flush the pot thoroughly with clean water two or three times a year to wash out what does build up.

How to Propagate Dragon Tree

Dracaena marginata propagates easily from cane cuttings, and this doubles as the way to deal with a plant that has grown too tall and bare. The plant regrows from the cut stump, so pruning and propagating are the same operation.

In spring, cut the cane wherever you want the plant to branch, using clean sharp secateurs. The stump will sprout two or three new shoots from just below the cut within a few weeks, making the parent bushier. The section you removed can be cut into pieces four to six inches long — keep track of which end was up, because a cutting planted upside down will not root. Stand the top cutting with its leaf rosette in water or damp mix; bare cane sections can be laid horizontally on damp mix or stood upright. Roots take three to six weeks in warmth and bright indirect light. Sealing the cut end of the parent with wax is sometimes recommended but is not necessary indoors.

Common Dragon Tree Problems & Fixes

Brown, dry leaf tips on almost every leaf

Usually means: Fluoride in tap water is the classic cause for this genus, with salt buildup and dry air contributing.

What to do: Switch to filtered, distilled or rainwater and flush the pot with clean water a few times a year. Trim the brown section at an angle, leaving a thin margin of dead tissue so you do not cut into living leaf.

Yellowing lower leaves dropping steadily

Usually means: Overwatering if widespread and the mix is damp. Losing the occasional lowest leaf as the cane grows taller is entirely normal for this species.

What to do: Extend the watering interval and check the pot drains. A bare lower cane with a tuft of leaves on top is the natural habit, not a fault.

Soft, mushy cane at soil level

Usually means: Stem rot from waterlogged soil, often after a winter of unchanged watering.

What to do: Cut the cane back above the rot into firm white tissue and root the healthy top as a cutting. The lower section is rarely salvageable once soft. Repot into fresh, faster-draining mix.

Pale or bleached patches on the leaves

Usually means: Direct sun through glass.

What to do: Move back from the window or filter the light. Scorched tissue does not recover; remove the worst leaves as new growth replaces them.

Canes leaning heavily to one side

Usually means: Phototropism — the plant is growing toward its light source — combined with not being rotated.

What to do: Rotate the pot a quarter turn every couple of weeks. An established bend will not straighten, but you can prune the cane and let it regrow upright.

Fine webbing between leaves in the crown

Usually means: Spider mites, which favour the tight rosette at the top of each cane.

What to do: Rinse the crown thoroughly in the shower and treat with insecticidal soap every five days for three cycles.

Small brown bumps along canes and leaf undersides

Usually means: Scale insects, well camouflaged against the woody cane.

What to do: Scrape them off with a cotton bud dipped in rubbing alcohol and follow with neem oil weekly for a month.

Repotting Dragon Tree

Dracaenas grow slowly and are happy pot-bound, so repot only every two to three years. The signals are roots circling the surface or through the drainage holes, and water running straight through without wetting the mix.

Repot in spring into a pot one size larger, choosing something heavy — a tall dracaena is top-heavy and light plastic tips easily. Loosen circling roots and trim any that are soft and brown.

For a large specimen that has outgrown practical repotting, top-dress instead: scrape off the top two or three inches of mix and replace with fresh compost each spring. Water well after repotting and hold off on fertiliser for two months.

Dragon Tree Varieties to Look For

Dracaena marginata has narrow green leaves with thin red margins. 'Tricolor' adds a cream stripe for a green, cream and pink effect and needs brighter light. 'Colorama' is dominated by broad red margins, is the most light-hungry of the three, and reverts toward green in shade.

'Bicolor' sits between the plain species and Tricolor. Related dracaenas worth knowing include the corn plant, with broad strappy leaves on thick canes, and 'Janet Craig', a solid deep green. All Dracaena species are toxic to cats and dogs, and cats in particular are attracted to the thin arching leaves — ingestion causes vomiting, drooling and dilated pupils in cats specifically.
