Parlor Palm Care in Depth

Light

Parlor palm is the classic Victorian parlour plant precisely because it copes with the dim interiors of the period. It handles low light better than almost any other palm and does well in medium indirect light, where growth is faster and fuller.

Keep it out of direct sun, which yellows and scorches the delicate fronds. An east or north window suits it, or a position a few feet back from a brighter one. In very low light expect slow growth and a somewhat sparse plant, but it will not decline the way most palms do in shade.

Watering through the year

Water when the top inch of mix has dried, then water thoroughly until it drains. In spring and summer that is usually every seven to ten days; in winter, every two weeks or so.

Parlor palms are more sensitive to overwatering than underwatering. The root system is fine and relatively small for the size of the plant, and it rots readily in soil that stays saturated. Empty the saucer after every watering.

They prefer humidity above 50% and show dry air as browning frond tips, which is the most common complaint. A pebble tray, a humidifier or grouping with other plants all help. They are also sensitive to fluoride in tap water, so filtered or rainwater is worth trying if tips keep browning.

Soil and potting mix

A standard peat-based potting mix with a handful of perlite works well. The mix should hold some moisture while draining freely — palms dislike both drought and waterlogging.

Note that parlor palms are almost always sold as several seedlings planted together in one pot to look full. That is normal and they should be left that way; separating them produces thin, sparse individual plants. Feed every six to eight weeks at half strength through spring and summer only, since palms are light feeders and prone to tip burn from excess fertiliser salts.

How to Propagate Parlor Palm

Parlor palm cannot be propagated from cuttings. It grows from a single growing point at the top of each stem, so a cut stem will not regrow and a severed frond will not root. This is true of most palms and is the thing most people get wrong.

The only genuine method is from seed, which is slow and difficult at home — germination takes months and requires consistent warmth and humidity, which is why almost all parlor palms are commercially grown. What you can do is separate the clump: since a shop plant is usually several individual seedlings potted together, you can carefully divide the root mass into two or three smaller clumps in spring. Keep at least three or four stems per division so the result still looks full, disturb the roots as little as possible, and keep the new pots warm and humid for a month. This multiplies your pots but does not produce new plants, and each division will be noticeably thinner than the original.

Common Parlor Palm Problems & Fixes

Brown frond tips

Usually means: The most common parlor palm complaint. Dry air, fluoride or salts in tap water, or inconsistent watering.

What to do: Raise humidity, switch to filtered or rainwater, and flush the pot with plain water every few months. Trim the brown tip only, following the frond's natural taper, and leave the green tissue intact.

Whole fronds yellowing from the bottom up

Usually means: Overwatering if the mix is wet, or natural ageing if it is only the oldest lower fronds one at a time.

What to do: Check the soil. Let the top inch dry before watering and confirm drainage. Cut spent fronds off at the base — they will not green up again.

Fronds pale and washed-out

Usually means: Too much light, or a nutrient shortage in a plant that has not been fed or repotted for several years.

What to do: Move out of direct sun. If light is already indirect, feed at half strength through the growing season and refresh the potting mix in spring.

Fine webbing between fronds, stippled grey foliage

Usually means: Spider mites, which are the signature parlor palm pest and thrive in dry indoor air.

What to do: Rinse the whole plant in the shower, including frond undersides, then treat with insecticidal soap every five days for three cycles. Raising humidity is what prevents recurrence.

Stems browning at soil level, plant collapsing

Usually means: Root rot from prolonged wet soil.

What to do: Unpot, cut away soft brown roots and any collapsed stems at the base, and repot into fresh free-draining mix. Water sparingly until new growth appears. Individual stems that have rotted will not recover.

No new growth for a long period

Usually means: Low light, cold, or an exhausted potting mix. Parlor palms are naturally slow, so some patience is warranted.

What to do: Move somewhere a little brighter and warmer, feed lightly through spring and summer, and repot every two to three years to refresh the mix.

Small brown bumps on stems and frond undersides

Usually means: Scale insects, which hide along the thin stems where they are easily missed.

What to do: Wipe them off with a cotton bud dipped in rubbing alcohol and follow with neem oil weekly for a month.

Repotting Parlor Palm

Parlor palms grow slowly and dislike root disturbance, so repot only every two to three years, or when roots are clearly emerging from the drainage holes. Spring is the best time.

Move up one pot size only. Keep the clump intact rather than separating the stems — the full appearance depends on them growing together. Handle the root ball gently; palm roots are brittle and damaged roots are slow to replace.

Set the plant at the same depth, water thoroughly, and keep it out of direct light and in decent humidity for a few weeks. Do not feed for six weeks. If the plant is large and repotting is impractical, top-dress instead by replacing the top two inches of mix each spring.

Parlor Palm Varieties to Look For

Chamaedorea elegans is the species sold as parlor palm and there are few named cultivars — 'Bella' is a compact form sometimes offered. Related species are worth knowing: Chamaedorea seifrizii is the bamboo palm, taller with narrower canes, and Chamaedorea metallica has broad blue-green leaves with a metallic sheen.

For a similar look with more height, the cat palm and kentia palm are both good choices, though kentia needs more light. Parlor palm is non-toxic to cats and dogs and appears on most pet-safe lists, which together with its shade tolerance makes it one of the most practical palms for an ordinary home.
