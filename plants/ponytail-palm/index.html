About the Ponytail Palm

Fun cascading leaves from a bulbous trunk. Stores water in its base.

Origin: Mexico

Care Tips

Bright light, water sparingly, very drought tolerant.

Ponytail Palm Care in Depth

Light

Ponytail palm wants bright light, including several hours of direct sun. It is not a palm at all but a succulent from the semi-desert of eastern Mexico, and it is adapted to open, exposed conditions. A south or west-facing window is the right position.

It tolerates medium light, where it simply grows very slowly and produces fewer new leaves. It will not thrive in a dim corner despite occasionally being sold as low-light tolerant. In good light the caudex — the swollen bulb at the base — thickens faster, which is the feature most people grow it for.

Watering through the year

This is the most drought-tolerant plant most people will own. The caudex is a water store, and a mature ponytail palm can go a month or more without water. Water deeply, then let the mix dry out completely — all the way through — before watering again.

In spring and summer that is typically every three to four weeks. In winter, every six to eight weeks is often sufficient, and in a cool room it can go longer still.

Overwatering is essentially the only way to kill this plant. A soft, squashy caudex means rot and is usually terminal by the time it is noticeable. When in doubt, do not water. Humidity is irrelevant; dry household air is exactly right.

Soil and potting mix

Use a cactus or succulent mix, or two parts potting compost to one part coarse sand and grit. It must drain fast and dry out fully between waterings.

Terracotta is strongly preferable, and the pot should be wide rather than deep, since the roots are shallow relative to the size of the caudex. Choose a snug pot: ponytail palms genuinely prefer being tight, and excess soil around a small root system stays wet. Feed at most two or three times across spring and summer with a half-strength succulent feed, and never in winter.

How to Propagate Ponytail Palm

Ponytail palm is propagated from offsets, and only from offsets — there is no way to root a cutting of the leaves or the caudex. Mature plants occasionally produce pups around the base of the trunk, though many never do, and a plant that has never offset cannot be forced.

When a pup is at least four inches tall and has begun developing its own roots, you can separate it. In spring, unpot the parent, brush the soil away and locate the join. Cut the pup away with a clean sharp knife, taking as much of its own root as possible, and let the cut surfaces dry and callus for several days in a shaded spot — this step is essential, and potting a fresh wound into soil reliably causes rot. Then plant in dry cactus mix and wait a week or more before the first light watering. Keep the pup in bright indirect light rather than full sun until it is established. Growth is slow: expect years, not months, for a pup to develop a noticeable caudex.

Common Ponytail Palm Problems & Fixes

Caudex soft or squashy to the touch

Usually means: Rot from overwatering. This is the one serious problem this plant has, and it is almost always advanced by the time it is felt.

What to do: Unpot immediately, cut away all soft tissue back to firm material, dust with sulphur if you have it, and let it dry for several days before repotting in dry gritty mix. Recovery depends entirely on how much firm caudex remains.

Brown, crispy leaf tips

Usually means: Normal on older leaves, but widespread browning indicates dry air, fluoride in tap water, or a long drought.

What to do: Trim the brown tips at an angle for appearance. Water more regularly if the mix has been dry for months, and try filtered water if tips keep browning.

Leaves yellowing and drooping from the base

Usually means: Overwatering if the mix is damp, or natural shedding of the oldest leaves as the plant grows.

What to do: Check the soil and extend the watering interval. Pull off spent lower leaves; a slightly bare stem below the ponytail is the natural habit.

Very little new growth for a year or more

Usually means: Low light, or simply the plant's natural pace. Ponytail palms are slow even in ideal conditions.

What to do: Move to the brightest position available and feed lightly in summer. Do not respond by watering more, which is the common and damaging mistake.

Leaves pale and washed-out

Usually means: Either too little light over a long period, or scorching from an abrupt move into intense sun.

What to do: Adjust position accordingly, increasing light gradually if moving it into a sunnier spot.

White cottony clusters where leaves meet the trunk

Usually means: Mealybugs, which shelter in the dense crown and among the leaf bases.

What to do: Dab with rubbing alcohol on a cotton bud and inspect weekly for a month. The crown gives them good cover, so be thorough.

Small flies around the pot

Usually means: Fungus gnats, which indicate the mix is being kept far too damp for this species.

What to do: Let the soil dry out completely and extend the watering interval substantially. A gritty top dressing discourages them.

Repotting Ponytail Palm

Ponytail palms need repotting rarely — every three to five years at most. They actively prefer being pot-bound, and a snug pot helps keep the mix dry. The usual prompt is the caudex physically filling the container.

Repot in spring into a pot only slightly larger, wide rather than deep, and heavy enough to balance a top-heavy plant. Terracotta suits it well.

Plant at the same depth — burying the caudex deeper than it was growing is a common mistake and leads directly to rot. Use dry mix and wait at least a week before watering. Skip fertiliser for a couple of months afterwards.

Ponytail Palm Varieties to Look For

Beaucarnea recurvata is the species, and there are no widely sold cultivars. What varies is form: single-trunk plants are the most common, while multi-headed specimens with two or three ponytails from one caudex are sold as a premium.

Beaucarnea guatemalensis is a close relative with a reddish tint to new growth. The plant is also sold as elephant's foot palm or bottle palm, and is sometimes mislabelled as Nolina recurvata, an older synonym. Ponytail palm is non-toxic to cats and dogs, which along with its extreme drought tolerance makes it one of the most practical plants for a pet household or for someone who travels frequently.
