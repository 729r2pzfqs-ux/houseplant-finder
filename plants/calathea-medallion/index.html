About the Calathea Medallion

Stunning patterned leaves that fold up at night. Beautiful but demanding.

Origin: Brazil

Care Tips

Indirect light, consistent moisture, high humidity, filtered water.

Calathea Medallion Care in Depth

Light

Calathea medallion grows on the forest floor in Brazilian rainforest, in light that has already been filtered through a canopy. Indoors it wants medium to bright indirect light and absolutely no direct sun, which bleaches the patterning and scorches the thin leaves within days.

A north-facing window is often ideal. An east window works if the plant sits back from the glass. In light that is too low the leaves lose contrast and the purple undersides dull, and the plant stops producing new growth — but low light is a far less common cause of calathea problems than water quality.

Watering through the year

Keep the mix evenly moist — never soggy, never dried out. Water when the top half-inch feels dry, which in spring and summer is usually every five to seven days. Reduce to every ten days or so in winter, but do not let the root ball dry out completely at any point.

Water quality matters more for calatheas than for almost any other houseplant. They are highly sensitive to fluoride, chlorine and dissolved salts, and hard tap water produces the crispy brown edges this genus is notorious for. Use filtered, distilled or rainwater, and the single most common calathea complaint largely disappears.

Humidity is the other non-negotiable. Calathea medallion wants 60% or more. A humidifier is the reliable answer; pebble trays and grouping help at the margins. Misting raises humidity for only minutes and wet leaves in still air invite fungal spotting, so it is not a substitute.

Soil and potting mix

Use a moisture-retentive but airy mix: roughly two parts peat or coir-based compost to one part perlite, with a little orchid bark. The aim is a medium that stays damp without compacting into a waterlogged block.

Plastic or glazed ceramic pots suit calatheas better than terracotta, which dries the root ball too quickly for a plant that wants consistent moisture. Drainage holes are still essential. Feed at quarter to half strength monthly through spring and summer only — calatheas are sensitive to fertiliser salts, and flushing the pot with clean water every couple of months prevents buildup.

How to Propagate Calathea Medallion

Calathea medallion is propagated by division only. Stem and leaf cuttings will not root, because the plant grows from a clumping rhizome rather than from nodes along a stem — a cut leaf simply rots.

Divide in spring, ideally while repotting, and only once the plant has formed a decent clump. Slide it from the pot and gently wash or tease the soil away so you can see where the rhizomes separate naturally. Pull the clump apart with your hands where possible, using a clean knife only where roots are too dense. Each division needs its own roots and at least two or three leaves; smaller pieces rarely establish. Pot each into fresh moisture-retentive mix, water with filtered water, and keep them warm, shaded and humid for the first three to four weeks — a clear bag loosely over the pot helps enormously. Expect the divisions to sulk and possibly drop a leaf before new growth begins.

Common Calathea Medallion Problems & Fixes

Brown, crispy leaf edges

Usually means: The classic calathea complaint. Usually minerals in tap water, low humidity, or both. Fertiliser salt buildup contributes.

What to do: Switch to filtered, distilled or rainwater, raise humidity to 60% with a humidifier, and flush the pot with clean water every couple of months. Trim damaged edges following the leaf's natural shape.

Leaves curling inward along the midrib

Usually means: Underwatering, low humidity, or the plant reacting to too much light. Some curling at night is normal — calatheas fold their leaves up in darkness, which is where the name prayer plant comes from.

What to do: Check the soil first. If dry, water. If moist and the curl persists during the day, raise humidity and move out of any direct light.

Yellowing leaves, soil constantly wet

Usually means: Overwatering and the beginnings of root rot.

What to do: Confirm the pot drains freely and is not sitting in water. Let the top half-inch dry before watering. If roots are brown and soft rather than firm, trim and repot into fresh mix.

Faded, washed-out patterning

Usually means: Too much light. Direct sun bleaches the markings and flattens the contrast that makes this plant worth growing.

What to do: Move to a shadier position with indirect light only. Faded leaves will not recover their pattern, but new growth will come in correctly marked.

Fine webbing and stippled, dull leaves

Usually means: Spider mites, which are the single most common calathea pest precisely because they thrive in the dry air that also causes brown edges.

What to do: Rinse the plant thoroughly, paying attention to leaf undersides, then treat with insecticidal soap every five days for three cycles. Fixing the humidity is what stops them returning.

Leaves drooping dramatically

Usually means: Either thirst or root rot — the symptom looks the same both ways, which catches people out.

What to do: Feel the soil before doing anything. Dry means water; wet means stop watering and check the roots immediately.

Brown or black spots with yellow halos

Usually means: Fungal leaf spot, usually after misting or water sitting on foliage in cool, still air.

What to do: Remove affected leaves, improve air circulation, stop misting, and water the soil rather than the leaves.

Repotting Calathea Medallion

Calatheas dislike root disturbance and are best repotted only every two years, or when roots fill the pot entirely and the plant dries out much faster than it used to. Spring is the right time, when the plant has the growing season ahead to recover.

Move up one size only, into a plastic or glazed pot that will hold moisture. Disturb the root ball as little as possible — loosen the outer roots and leave the core intact rather than washing the soil away, unless you are dividing.

Water with filtered water afterwards, keep humidity high, and expect a period of sulking. Do not fertilise for six to eight weeks. If your calathea is otherwise healthy, leaving it alone is usually better than repotting on schedule; this is not a plant that appreciates routine disturbance.

Calathea Medallion Varieties to Look For

Calathea veitchiana 'Medallion' has large rounded leaves with feathered green patterning and deep burgundy undersides. Calathea orbifolia is bigger, with broad silver-striped leaves and a reputation for being fussier still. Calathea lancifolia, the rattlesnake plant, has wavy lance-shaped leaves with dark blotches and is among the most forgiving in the genus.

Calathea 'White Fusion' is the most demanding of the commonly sold forms, with white variegation that browns readily if humidity drops. Note that many calatheas have been reclassified as Goeppertia, so you may see either name on a label. All calatheas are non-toxic to cats and dogs, which makes them a popular pet-household choice despite the care demands.
