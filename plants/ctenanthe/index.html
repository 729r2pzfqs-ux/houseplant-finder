About the Ctenanthe

A gorgeous prayer plant relative with striking striped leaves. More forgiving than Calathea but equally beautiful.

Origin: Brazil

Care Tips

Medium indirect light, keep soil consistently moist, appreciates humidity.

Ctenanthe Care in Depth

Light

Ctenanthe wants medium to bright indirect light with no direct sun. It is a prayer plant relative from Brazilian forest floors and is adapted to filtered light.

A north or east-facing window suits it well. Direct sun bleaches the feathered markings and scorches the leaves. In light that is too low the patterning loses contrast and the plant becomes leggy, since Ctenanthe has a more upright, branching habit than calathea and stretches more visibly. Rotate the pot every few weeks for even growth.

Watering through the year

Keep the mix evenly moist — never soggy, never dry. Water when the top half-inch feels dry, usually every five to seven days in spring and summer and every ten days in winter.

Like the rest of the prayer plant family, Ctenanthe is sensitive to fluoride, chlorine and dissolved salts in tap water, which produce brown crispy edges. Use filtered, distilled or rainwater. Use room-temperature water rather than cold, which this family dislikes.

Humidity above 60% is genuinely needed. That said, Ctenanthe is somewhat more tolerant of ordinary household air than calathea, which makes it a reasonable alternative for someone who has struggled with that genus. A humidifier still improves it considerably. The leaves fold upward at night and open by morning, as with all Marantaceae.

Soil and potting mix

Use a moisture-retentive but airy mix — two parts coir or peat-based compost to one part perlite with a little fine bark. It should stay damp without compacting.

Plastic or glazed ceramic pots hold moisture better than terracotta. Drainage holes are still essential. Feed at quarter to half strength monthly through spring and summer only, and flush the pot with clean water every couple of months to clear salts.

How to Propagate Ctenanthe

Ctenanthe is propagated by division, and it is somewhat easier than calathea because the plant produces distinct offshoots from a spreading rhizome rather than a single dense clump.

Divide in spring, ideally while repotting. Slide the plant from its pot and look for natural sections where a cluster of stems has its own roots. Ctenanthe spreads by rhizome and these sections often separate with minimal cutting.

Each division needs its own roots and at least three or four leaves. Pot into fresh moisture-retentive mix, water with filtered water at room temperature, and keep warm, shaded and humid for the first month under a clear cover. Expect some sulking and possibly a lost leaf before new growth begins. Unlike calathea, Ctenanthe's more branching habit means a mature plant often divides into several good-sized pieces rather than two.

Common Ctenanthe Problems & Fixes

Brown crispy leaf edges

Usually means: Minerals in tap water, low humidity, or fertiliser salt buildup.

What to do: Switch to filtered, distilled or rainwater, raise humidity above 60%, and flush the pot with clean water every couple of months. Trim damaged edges following the leaf shape.

Leaves curling and not opening flat during the day

Usually means: Underwatering, low humidity, or too much light. Folding upward at night is normal.

What to do: Check the soil first, then humidity, then position. Leaves usually resume their daily rhythm within a week of conditions improving.

Faded feathered markings

Usually means: Too much light, usually direct sun.

What to do: Move to a shadier position. Existing faded leaves stay as they are; new growth comes in properly marked.

Leggy stems with bare stretches

Usually means: Insufficient light. Ctenanthe's upright branching habit makes stretching more visible than in clumping calatheas.

What to do: Move to brighter indirect light and cut back the leggiest stems in spring. The plant branches from the base.

Yellowing leaves with wet soil

Usually means: Overwatering and root rot.

What to do: Confirm the pot drains and is not standing in water. Let the top half-inch dry before each watering.

Brown tips despite filtered water and good humidity

Usually means: Cold water or cold draughts — this family is sensitive to temperature shocks.

What to do: Water with room-temperature water and move the plant away from draughty windows and doors.

Fine webbing under the leaves

Usually means: Spider mites, drawn by dry air.

What to do: Rinse thoroughly, treat with insecticidal soap every five days for three cycles, and fix the humidity.

Repotting Ctenanthe

Repot every two years in spring, or when roots have filled the pot. Ctenanthe spreads by rhizome and fills a pot sideways, so look for roots and new shoots crowding the surface.

Choose a wide pot one size larger, in plastic or glazed ceramic to retain moisture. Disturb the root ball as little as possible unless you are dividing.

Water with room-temperature filtered water, keep humidity high for a few weeks, and do not feed for six weeks. This is the natural moment to divide a spreading clump, which also rejuvenates a plant that has become bare in the centre.

Ctenanthe Varieties to Look For

Ctenanthe burle-marxii, the fishbone prayer plant, has pale green leaves with dark feathered bands and purple undersides. Ctenanthe setosa 'Grey Star' is larger with silvery-grey leaves. Ctenanthe oppenheimiana 'Tricolor', the never-never plant, has cream and green variegation with deep maroon undersides.

Within the prayer plant family, Calathea is the most demanding, Maranta the easiest to propagate since it roots from nodes, and Stromanthe sits between them. Ctenanthe is generally regarded as one of the more forgiving members and a good stepping stone. All Marantaceae, including Ctenanthe, are non-toxic to cats and dogs.
