About the Golden Pothos

One of the easiest houseplants to grow. Trailing vines with heart-shaped leaves. Thrives in almost any condition.

Origin: Southeast Asia

Care Tips

Low to bright indirect light, water when dry, very forgiving.

Golden Pothos Care in Depth

Light

Golden pothos is genuinely adaptable, which is why it turns up in offices and windowless bathrooms. It grows in anything from low light to bright indirect light. What changes with light levels is not survival but appearance: in bright indirect light the leaves are larger and the golden variegation is pronounced, while in low light new leaves come out smaller and mostly green.

Avoid direct midday sun, which scorches the foliage and bleaches the variegation to a washed-out cream. If your plant is losing its gold, move it brighter before assuming it is a watering problem.

Watering through the year

Let the top one to two inches of mix dry before watering, then water thoroughly until it drains. In a moderately bright spot during spring and summer this tends to be every seven to ten days, less in lower light where the mix stays damp longer.

Reduce through autumn and winter, often to every two to three weeks. Pothos is forgiving of a missed watering and distinctly unforgiving of a permanently wet pot, so when in doubt, wait.

The plant gives clear signals: leaves go slightly limp and soft when thirsty and perk up within hours of watering. Use that as your guide rather than a fixed schedule, but do not let wilting become routine — repeated severe drought causes permanent leaf loss along the vine.

Soil and potting mix

An ordinary well-draining houseplant mix works. Adding a handful of perlite or orchid bark improves aeration and makes overwatering harder to achieve, which is worth doing if you tend to water generously.

Pothos also grows indefinitely in plain water, which is why cuttings in jars do so well. A plant grown permanently in water needs a weak dose of liquid fertiliser every month or so, since there are no nutrients in the water itself. In soil, feed monthly at half strength through spring and summer only.

How to Propagate Golden Pothos

Pothos is one of the easiest houseplants to propagate, and it is a good first attempt if you have never taken a cutting. Find a node — the small brown nub on the vine where a leaf stem joins, often with a stubby aerial root already showing — and cut half an inch below it. Each cutting needs at least one node and one leaf.

Stand the cuttings in a glass of water with the nodes submerged and the leaves above the surface, in bright indirect light. Change the water weekly to keep it oxygenated. Roots normally appear within one to two weeks, and once they reach two to three inches you can pot several cuttings together into one pot for a full-looking plant straight away. Keep newly potted cuttings a little moister than an established plant for the first couple of weeks. Cuttings can equally be pushed straight into damp soil, skipping water entirely; the rooting is slower to watch but the plant adapts faster.

Common Golden Pothos Problems & Fixes

Yellow leaves, often several at once

Usually means: Overwatering is the usual cause, particularly if the mix is still wet and the yellowing starts at the base of the plant.

What to do: Let the top inches dry properly before the next watering and empty any water standing in the cachepot. One yellow leaf occasionally is normal ageing and not a concern.

Brown, crispy leaf tips and edges

Usually means: Dry air, underwatering, or accumulated fertiliser salts and chlorine from tap water.

What to do: Water more consistently, flush the pot with plain water every few months, and consider filtered water if your tap water is heavily treated.

Long bare vines with widely spaced leaves

Usually means: Too little light. The plant stretches toward the nearest source, producing distance between leaves rather than new foliage.

What to do: Move it brighter and prune the bare vines back to within a few inches of the soil. Pothos branches readily from pruned nodes, and the cuttings you removed can be rooted and added back to the same pot.

Variegation fading to plain green

Usually means: Insufficient light. The gold-marked tissue contains less chlorophyll, so in dim conditions the plant favours green growth.

What to do: Increase light gradually. Cut back fully green vines to encourage variegated growth from lower nodes.

Black or dark brown stem bases, plant collapsing

Usually means: Root rot from prolonged wet soil, sometimes with a sour smell from the mix.

What to do: Take healthy cuttings from the upper vines immediately as insurance, then unpot, trim all soft black roots, and repot into fresh mix in a pot with drainage. Water sparingly until new growth appears.

Small flies rising from the soil

Usually means: Fungus gnats, which breed in permanently damp topsoil and are a symptom of watering too often.

What to do: Let the top layer dry fully between waterings, use yellow sticky traps for the adults, and water from below for a few weeks. The problem resolves once the surface stops staying wet.

Leaves limp and drooping despite damp soil

Usually means: Root rot rather than thirst. Damaged roots cannot take up water, so the plant wilts even in wet mix.

What to do: Check the roots. Healthy roots are firm and white; rotted ones are brown and fall apart. Trim, repot into fresh mix, and reduce watering frequency.

Repotting Golden Pothos

Pothos grows fast and generally needs a new pot every one to two years. Signs are roots emerging from the drainage holes, water running straight through, or growth slowing noticeably during the growing season.

Move up one size, about two inches wider. Spring is best but pothos is tolerant enough to repot at almost any time. Tease apart circling roots at the base of the root ball so they grow outward rather than continuing to spiral.

If the plant has gone bare and leggy at the top, repotting is a good moment to cut it back hard and replant rooted cuttings around the parent to thicken it up. Water well afterwards and wait a month before resuming fertiliser.

Golden Pothos Varieties to Look For

'Golden' is the familiar green-and-yellow form. 'Marble Queen' is heavily streaked with white and grows more slowly, since it carries less chlorophyll. 'Neon' is a uniform chartreuse with no variegation at all and keeps its colour best in bright indirect light.

'N'Joy' and 'Pearls and Jade' are compact, with distinct white-bordered leaves. 'Satin pothos' is sold alongside these but is actually Scindapsus pictus, a different genus with velvety, silver-spotted leaves — care is very similar. Note that all pothos varieties are toxic to cats and dogs if chewed, so a hanging position is worth considering in a pet household.
