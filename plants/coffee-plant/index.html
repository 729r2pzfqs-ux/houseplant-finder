Coffee Plant Care in Depth

Light

Coffee plant wants bright indirect light. In its native Ethiopian highlands it grows as an understorey shrub beneath taller trees, so it is adapted to filtered light rather than full exposure.

An east window is ideal, or a few feet back from a south or west window. Direct sun scorches the glossy leaves, producing brown patches and yellowing — this is the most common mistake, since people assume a tropical crop plant wants full sun. In low light it survives but grows slowly and will not flower. Rotate the pot regularly for even growth.

Watering through the year

Keep the mix consistently moist but never soggy, watering when the top inch is dry — usually every five to seven days in spring and summer. Coffee dislikes drying out and responds by dropping leaves.

Reduce to every ten to fourteen days in winter, but never let the root ball dry through. The balance is evenly damp; both drought and waterlogging cause leaf loss.

Humidity above 50% is important. Coffee comes from a misty highland climate and dry indoor air produces browning leaf edges and invites spider mites. A humidifier helps considerably. Use filtered or rainwater if your tap water is hard, since coffee prefers slightly acidic conditions and is sensitive to accumulated salts.

Soil and potting mix

Use a slightly acidic, well-draining potting mix — a peat or coir-based compost with added perlite works well, and an ericaceous compost blended with perlite is even better. Coffee prefers a pH around 6 to 6.5.

Drainage holes are essential. Feed every two to four weeks through spring and summer with a balanced fertiliser at half strength, ideally one formulated for acid-loving plants. Coffee is a moderately hungry grower. Stop feeding in winter. Flush the pot with clean water every couple of months to prevent salt buildup.

How to Propagate Coffee Plant

Coffee propagates from seed and from stem cuttings, with an important caveat about seed that catches people out.

Seed must be genuinely fresh — green, unroasted coffee beans still in their parchment. Roasted supermarket beans will never germinate, and even green beans lose viability within a few months. Fresh seed sown in damp mix at 25°C germinates in two to four months, which requires patience.

Stem cuttings are more practical. In spring or early summer, take a cutting six to eight inches long from a healthy upright stem — and specifically from a vertical shoot rather than a side branch, because cuttings from lateral branches grow permanently sideways and never form an upright plant. Remove the lower leaves, dip in rooting hormone, and pot into damp free-draining mix under a clear bag. Keep warm and humid in bright indirect light. Roots take two to three months, which is slower than most houseplants.

Common Coffee Plant Problems & Fixes

Brown patches and yellowing on leaves

Usually means: Direct sun. Coffee is an understorey plant and scorches readily, contrary to what most people assume.

What to do: Move out of direct light into bright filtered conditions. Damaged leaves do not recover.

Leaves dropping suddenly

Usually means: The soil dried out, a cold draught, or a sharp temperature change. Coffee is sensitive to temperatures below about 15°C.

What to do: Keep the mix evenly moist, move away from draughty windows and doors, and maintain a warm stable position.

Brown crispy leaf edges

Usually means: Low humidity or salt buildup from fertiliser and hard water.

What to do: Raise humidity above 50%, flush the pot with clean water every couple of months, and use filtered water.

Yellowing leaves with green veins

Usually means: Iron deficiency, common in coffee when the soil pH has drifted too alkaline — often from hard tap water.

What to do: Repot into an acidic mix, use filtered or rainwater, and feed with a fertiliser for acid-loving plants containing chelated iron.

No flowers or berries

Usually means: The plant is too young — coffee needs three to five years before flowering — or light and humidity are insufficient.

What to do: Be patient, maintain bright indirect light and good humidity, and feed through the growing season. Indoor plants do flower and fruit, but it takes years.

Fine webbing and stippled leaves

Usually means: Spider mites, encouraged by dry indoor air.

What to do: Rinse the plant thoroughly, treat with insecticidal soap every five days for three cycles, and raise humidity.

Leggy stem with leaves only at the top

Usually means: Insufficient light and no pruning.

What to do: Increase light and prune the top back in spring to encourage branching. Prune vertical shoots rather than side branches if you plan to root the cuttings.

Repotting Coffee Plant

Repot every one to two years in spring while the plant is growing, then less often. Signs are roots through the drainage holes and rapid drying between waterings.

Move up one pot size into fresh slightly acidic, free-draining mix. Coffee has a deep taproot when young, so a pot with reasonable depth suits it better than a shallow one.

Handle the roots gently and water thoroughly afterwards. Keep humidity up and out of direct sun for a couple of weeks while it settles. Do not feed for six weeks. Prune to shape at the same time if the plant has grown leggy.

Coffee Plant Varieties to Look For

Coffea arabica is the species grown as a houseplant, with glossy dark green leaves and, on mature plants, fragrant white flowers followed by red berries. 'Nana' is a dwarf cultivar better suited to indoor growing, staying around three feet.

Coffea canephora, robusta coffee, is occasionally sold but is larger and less ornamental. Two practical notes: harvesting usable coffee from a houseplant is possible but a mature indoor plant produces only a handful of beans a year, so grow it for the foliage rather than the crop. And all parts of the coffee plant except the ripe berry flesh are toxic to cats and dogs due to caffeine content, causing vomiting, rapid heartbeat and restlessness.
