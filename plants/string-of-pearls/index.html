Unique trailing succulent with bead-like leaves. Stunning in hanging baskets.

Origin: South Africa

Care Tips

Bright indirect light, water sparingly, well-draining soil.

String of Pearls Care in Depth

Light

String of pearls needs bright light — more than most people give it. Several hours of direct morning sun plus bright indirect light for the rest of the day is close to ideal. A south or east-facing window is usually right.

In insufficient light the strands stretch, the pearls space out widely along bare stems, and the plant looks sparse rather than dense. This is the single most common reason string of pearls disappoints. Harsh afternoon sun through glass can scorch the beads, so filter the strongest midday light in summer while keeping overall levels high.

Watering through the year

Treat it as the succulent it is. Water thoroughly, then let the mix dry out almost completely before watering again — typically every two to three weeks in spring and summer, and once a month or less in winter.

The pearls themselves are the gauge. Plump and firm means well watered; slightly flattened or shrivelled means it is time. Mushy, translucent pearls that burst at a touch mean serious overwatering, and by then root rot is usually established.

Water at the soil surface rather than over the strands, and drain completely. The shallow root system sits near the surface, so a deep soak followed by a long dry period suits it far better than frequent light watering. Humidity is irrelevant; dry household air is fine.

Soil and potting mix

Use a gritty cactus or succulent mix, or two parts succulent compost to one part perlite or coarse sand. Fast drainage is essential — this plant rots faster than almost any other common houseplant in soil that stays damp.

String of pearls has a shallow, fine root system, so a wide shallow pot suits it much better than a deep one, which holds a reservoir of wet soil below the roots. Terracotta helps. Feed sparingly: a half-strength succulent fertiliser twice during the growing season is ample, none in winter.

How to Propagate String of Pearls

String of pearls is very easy to propagate, which is fortunate because plants tend to thin at the crown over time and benefit from being refreshed with new cuttings.

Take a strand four to six inches long and remove the pearls from the last inch or two to expose the bare stem. You can then either lay the strand flat on the surface of damp cactus mix and press the bare section lightly into contact — roots form at each node where stem touches soil — or insert the bare end an inch into the mix. The laying method is more productive, because a single strand roots at multiple points and produces a fuller plant. Let cut ends callus for a day first. Keep the mix barely moist, not wet, and in bright indirect light; roots take two to three weeks. Planting several rooted cuttings around the edge of the parent pot is the standard way to thicken a plant that has gone bare in the middle. Avoid water propagation, which works poorly and often rots the strands.

Common String of Pearls Problems & Fixes

Pearls shrivelling and going flat

Usually means: Underwatering, or occasionally root rot preventing water uptake despite damp soil.

What to do: Check the soil. If dry, water thoroughly and the pearls plump up within days. If the soil is wet and the pearls are still shrivelled, the roots have failed — take healthy cuttings and restart.

Pearls turning mushy and bursting

Usually means: Overwatering. The pearls take up more water than they can hold and the cells rupture.

What to do: Stop watering and let the mix dry out completely. Check for rot at the crown and take cuttings from healthy strands as insurance. Repot into grittier mix if the soil holds water too long.

Bare stems with pearls far apart

Usually means: Not enough light. The strands stretch toward the light source instead of producing dense growth.

What to do: Move to a much brighter position. Stretched sections will not fill in, so trim them and use the cuttings to replant into the same pot.

Plant thinning and dying back in the middle

Usually means: Natural ageing of the crown, worsened by water collecting among the strands at the centre.

What to do: This is normal for mature plants. Take cuttings from the healthy outer strands and replant them around the crown, or start a fresh pot. Water at the soil edge rather than over the centre.

Brown or scorched patches on individual pearls

Usually means: Sunburn from intense direct afternoon sun, especially through glass.

What to do: Filter the strongest midday light while keeping overall brightness high. Damaged pearls do not recover and can be trimmed away.

White cottony clusters among the strands

Usually means: Mealybugs, which hide among the dense pearls and are hard to spot until numerous.

What to do: Dab each cluster with rubbing alcohol on a cotton bud. Check thoroughly every week for a month — the tangled strands give them excellent cover.

Whole strands going brown and dry from the base

Usually means: Crown or stem rot at soil level, usually from overwatering or water sitting against the stems.

What to do: Cut away affected strands entirely, let the soil dry, and salvage healthy strands as cuttings. Improve drainage and water only at the pot edge.

Repotting String of Pearls

String of pearls has a small, shallow root system and is happy pot-bound, so repot only every two to three years, or when the mix has broken down and stopped draining.

Choose a wide, shallow pot rather than a deep one — excess soil below the shallow roots simply stays wet. Terracotta or another breathable material helps. Spring is the right time.

Handle the strands carefully; they detach easily and every broken strand is a cutting worth saving. Use dry mix, wait a week before the first watering, and skip fertiliser for a couple of months. Repotting is a good moment to tuck rooted cuttings into any bare areas of the pot.

String of Pearls Varieties to Look For

Curio rowleyanus, formerly Senecio rowleyanus, is the true string of pearls with spherical beads. A variegated form with cream-marbled pearls exists and is slower, more delicate and needs strong light.

Close relatives with identical care include string of bananas, with crescent-shaped leaves and a reputation for being noticeably tougher, string of dolphins, whose leaves curve into little dolphin shapes, and string of tears, with more teardrop-shaped beads. All of these Curio species are toxic to cats and dogs, and because the trailing strands are so attractive to a playful cat, a high shelf or hanging position well out of reach is genuinely important.
