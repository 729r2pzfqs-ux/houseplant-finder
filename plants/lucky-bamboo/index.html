About the Lucky Bamboo

Not actually bamboo. Grows in water or soil. Very resilient.

Origin: Central Africa

Care Tips

Low to medium light, keep in water or moist soil, change water regularly.

Lucky Bamboo Care in Depth

Light

Lucky bamboo is not bamboo at all — it is Dracaena sanderiana, and it wants the same conditions as other dracaenas: bright indirect light, never direct sun.

It tolerates quite low light, which is why it survives on office desks, though growth slows and the leaves lose colour. Direct sun scorches the leaves and turns them yellow quickly. If your stalks are pale and the leaves yellowing at the tips, check for sun exposure before assuming it is a water problem. Rotate the container so the stalks grow straight rather than curving toward one side.

Watering through the year

Lucky bamboo is usually grown in water rather than soil, and the water is where almost every problem starts. Keep the roots covered by one to two inches of water — no more — and change it completely every one to two weeks. Stale water is what turns stalks yellow and causes the slimy root rot this plant is prone to.

The critical point is water quality. Dracaena sanderiana is highly sensitive to chlorine and especially fluoride, and ordinary tap water causes brown, burnt leaf tips within weeks. Use filtered, distilled or rainwater, or leave tap water standing uncovered for 24 hours to let chlorine dissipate — though this does not remove fluoride.

If grown in soil, keep the mix consistently moist but not waterlogged, watering when the surface feels barely dry. Soil-grown plants are generally longer-lived and more robust than water-grown ones. Humidity above 40% is appreciated.

Soil and potting mix

In water culture, use clean pebbles or glass beads to hold the stalks upright, filling the container so the roots are submerged and the stalks are not. Rinse the pebbles when you change the water.

If growing in soil, use a well-draining potting mix with added perlite. Soil culture suits the plant better long term and produces stronger growth. Feed very sparingly either way — a single drop of liquid fertiliser diluted in the water every couple of months is plenty for water-grown plants, and a half-strength feed every two months for soil-grown ones. Over-feeding causes yellowing and algae.

How to Propagate Lucky Bamboo

Lucky bamboo propagates readily from stem cuttings, and the technique differs slightly from other dracaenas because of how the stalks are grown.

Look for an offshoot — a side shoot growing from the main stalk. Cut it off where it joins, leaving a short stub, and stand it in clean water. Roots appear within three to five weeks. Alternatively, cut a main stalk into sections, each with at least one node, keeping track of which end was up; a section planted upside down will not root.

Seal the top cut of the parent stalk with wax or a little paraffin to prevent rot and moisture loss — this is standard practice with lucky bamboo and worth doing. Keep new cuttings in filtered water in bright indirect light, changing it weekly. Once roots are two inches long, the cutting can continue in water or be potted into soil. Note that the spiral shapes sold commercially are produced by rotating the plant beside a light source over months, not by any genetic trait, so cuttings grow straight.

Common Lucky Bamboo Problems & Fixes

Brown, burnt leaf tips

Usually means: Fluoride and chlorine in tap water. This is the single most common lucky bamboo complaint.

What to do: Switch to filtered, distilled or rainwater immediately and change the water every one to two weeks. Trim the brown tips at an angle. New growth will be clean.

Stalk turning yellow from the bottom up

Usually means: Stale water, too much fertiliser, or direct sun. Yellowing that starts at the base usually means rot.

What to do: Remove the affected stalk from the arrangement so it does not contaminate the others. If only the lower portion is yellow, cut above it into green tissue and re-root the healthy top.

Slimy roots and cloudy, smelly water

Usually means: Bacterial rot from water left too long without changing.

What to do: Rinse the roots and pebbles thoroughly, trim any mushy roots, and refill with fresh filtered water. Change water every week thereafter. Discard badly affected stalks.

Yellow leaves while the stalk stays green

Usually means: Over-fertilising, too much light, or fluoride in the water.

What to do: Stop feeding, move out of direct sun, and switch to filtered water. Remove yellow leaves cleanly.

Algae growing on the pebbles and container walls

Usually means: Light reaching the water in a clear glass container, combined with nutrients from fertiliser.

What to do: Clean the container and pebbles thoroughly, reduce feeding, and consider an opaque container or one that keeps light off the water.

No new growth for a long period

Usually means: Low light, or a plant grown in water for years that has exhausted what water culture can provide.

What to do: Move somewhere brighter and feed very lightly. Potting into soil often revitalises a plant that has stalled in water after several years.

Stalk soft and mushy

Usually means: Rot, usually from stale water or from the stalk being submerged rather than just the roots.

What to do: Remove that stalk immediately. Keep water level below the stalk bases — only the roots should be submerged. Salvage healthy tops as cuttings.

Repotting Lucky Bamboo

For water-grown plants, there is no repotting as such, but the container should be emptied, rinsed and refilled with fresh water every one to two weeks, and the pebbles washed occasionally. This is the maintenance that keeps the plant alive.

Move a water-grown plant into soil every few years if it has stalled — many lucky bamboos decline after two or three years in water alone, and potting into a well-draining mix often revives them. Do this in spring, keeping the soil consistently moist for the first month while the water roots adapt.

For soil-grown plants, repot every two to three years into a pot one size larger, handling the roots gently and setting the stalks at the same depth.

Lucky Bamboo Varieties to Look For

Dracaena sanderiana is the species. What varies commercially is the arrangement rather than the plant: straight stalks, spirals, woven lattices and tiered towers are all produced by manipulating the growing plant next to a light source over months.

'Victory' has broader variegated leaves and 'Golden' has yellow-striped foliage. The number of stalks carries meaning in feng shui tradition — three for happiness, five for wealth, eight for growth — with four generally avoided. Important safety note: despite the name and its reputation as a gift plant, Dracaena sanderiana is toxic to cats and dogs, and cats are particularly affected. It is frequently assumed to be safe because true bamboo is; it is not.
